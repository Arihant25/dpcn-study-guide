MThe maths you need, from the beginning

The course uses a small set of maths tools again and again. Each one is explained here with an example. You do not need anything beyond this page.

M1 · Averages and the angle brackets ⟨ ⟩

Angle brackets around something mean "the average of it". If five people have 2, 3, 3, 2 and 2 friends, the average number of friends is

⟨k⟩=2+3+3+2+25=125=2.4.

⟨k2⟩ means "square each number first, then average": (4+9+9+4+4)/5=30/5=6. Note that ⟨k2⟩ (6) is not the same as ⟨k⟩2 (2.42=5.76). The difference between them measures how spread out the numbers are. It is called the variance:

var⁡(k)=⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩2=6−5.76=0.24.

If every number were the same, the variance would be 0. The square root of the variance is the standard deviation σ (Greek "sigma"), a typical distance from the average.

M2 · The summation sign Σ

∑ (capital Greek "sigma") means "add up". The small text under and over it says what to add.

∑k=14k=1+2+3+4=10,∑k=13k2=1+4+9=14.

Two sums that come up in path-length questions: 1+2+⋯+n=n(n+1)2 and 12+22+⋯+n2=n(n+1)(2n+1)6. Check the first with n=4: 4×5/2=10.

An average can be written with a sum. If P(k) is the fraction of people with k friends, then ⟨k⟩=∑kkP(k). In the example, 3 out of 5 have 2 friends and 2 out of 5 have 3, so ⟨k⟩=2×35+3×25=1.2+1.2=2.4, the same as before.

M3 · Probability

  • A probability is a number from 0 (never happens) to 1 (always happens). A fair coin lands heads with probability 12.
  • "Not": if something happens with probability p, it fails to happen with probability 1−p.
  • "And" for independent events: multiply. Two heads in a row: 12×12=14. "Independent" means one result does not affect the other.
  • "Or" for events that cannot both happen: add. A die shows 1 or 2 with probability 16+16=13.
  • The expected value is the long-run average. If you flip a coin 10 times, the expected number of heads is 10×12=5. In general, n tries each succeeding with probability p give np successes on average.
  • (nk), read "n choose k", counts the ways to pick k things out of n. (42)=6: from A, B, C, D the pairs are AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD. The formula is (nk)=n!k!(n−k)!, where n!=n×(n−1)×⋯×1. The number of pairs among N nodes is (N2)=N(N−1)2.

M4 · Powers

k3=k×k×k. A negative power means "one over": k−2=1k2, so 2−2=14 and 10−2=0.01. Rules: ka×kb=ka+b and kakb=ka−b. For large k, k−2 is tiny, k0=1 always, and k2 is huge. So a large number raised to a negative power goes towards 0, and raised to a positive power grows without limit.

M5 · The number e, exponentials and ln

e≈2.718 is a fixed number, like π. ex grows fast as x increases, and e−x shrinks towards 0: e−1≈0.37, e−2≈0.14, e−5≈0.007. Also e0=1.

ln⁡ (the natural logarithm) undoes e: ln⁡(ex)=x. It turns multiplying into adding and powers into multiplying: ln⁡(ab)=ln⁡a+ln⁡b, ln⁡(an)=nln⁡a. It grows very slowly: ln⁡10≈2.3, ln⁡1000≈6.9, ln⁡106≈13.8. The base-10 logarithm log10⁡ works the same way, with log10⁡100=2.

Three approximations are used in the derivations. You can use them without proof.

  1. For large n, (1−xn)n≈e−x. Check with x=1, n=100: 0.99100=0.366, and e−1=0.368.
  2. For small x, e−x≈1−x+x22. Check with x=0.1: e−0.1=0.9048, and 1−0.1+0.005=0.905.
  3. For small p, ln⁡(1−p)≈−p. Check: ln⁡0.99=−0.01005.

M6 · Derivatives (slopes) and "dot" notation

The derivative of a function is its slope: how fast it goes up or down. f′(x) or dfdx both mean "the slope of f at x". A positive slope means the curve is going up, negative means going down.

  • Slope of xn is nxn−1. So x2 has slope 2x, x3 has slope 3x2, and 5x has slope 5. A constant has slope 0.
  • Slope of ecx is cecx.
  • Slopes of a sum add: the slope of rx−x2 is r−2x.

A dot over a letter means "rate of change over time". x˙=dxdt. If x is a population, x˙=3 means it is growing by 3 per unit of time.

M7 · Integrals (adding up continuous things)

An integral ∫abf(k)dk is the area under the curve f(k) between k=a and k=b. You can think of it as a sum ∑ over a smooth range of values instead of whole numbers. The course replaces sums over degrees by integrals because integrals are easier to work out. Your syllabus note "not discrete!" refers to this.

The only rule you need is the reverse of the power rule for slopes:

∫kadk=ka+1a+1(for a≠−1).

To use limits, work out the answer at the top limit and subtract its value at the bottom limit. This is written with square brackets:

∫12k2dk=[k33]12=83−13=73.

When the top limit is infinity

Power-law questions integrate up to ∞ (infinity). The answer depends on whether ka+1 shrinks or grows as k gets huge (see M4).

  • ∫1∞k−2dk=[k−1−1]1∞=[−1k]1∞=0−(−1)=1. At k=∞, 1/k is 0. The area is finite.
  • ∫1∞k−1/2dk=[2k1/2]1∞. At k=∞, k is infinite. The area is infinite. We say the integral diverges.

So the integral up to infinity is finite only when the power a+1 is negative. This single fact decides every "when does it diverge?" question in §1.

M8 · The one differential equation you must know

A differential equation tells you the rate of change of something in terms of its current value. The simplest one says "the rate of growth is proportional to the current amount":

y˙=ay⟹y(t)=y(0)eat

You can check it: the slope of y(0)eat is ay(0)eat=ay. If a>0, y grows exponentially. If a<0, y shrinks to 0. Almost every stability question in Parts 3 and 4 comes down to "is this a positive or negative?".

M9 · Matrices, eigenvectors and eigenvalues

A matrix is a table of numbers. A vector is a single column of numbers. Multiplying a matrix by a vector: each entry of the answer is a row of the matrix multiplied entry by entry with the vector, then added.

(2112)(11)=(2⋅1+1⋅11⋅1+2⋅1)=(33)=3(11).

Here the matrix did not change the direction of (1,1), it only stretched it by 3. Such a special vector is an eigenvector, and the stretch factor 3 is its eigenvalue. Written generally, M𝐯=λ𝐯 (λ is Greek "lambda"). The same matrix has a second eigenvector (1,−1), with eigenvalue 1, since the result is (2−1, 1−2)=(1,−1).

Why this matters: in Part 4, a network's behaviour is split into these special directions. Along each eigenvector, the problem becomes the single equation y˙=ay of M8, which we can solve. For a symmetric matrix (one that equals its mirror image across the diagonal), the eigenvectors are at right angles to each other ("orthogonal"), which is what lets the problem split cleanly.

0Network words

A network is a set of things (people, computers, web pages) and the connections between them. These are the words used to describe one. We use one small example throughout.

  • A node (also called a vertex) is one of the things. N is the number of nodes. Here N=5.
  • A link (also called an edge) is a connection between two nodes. L is the number of links. Here L=6.
  • Two nodes joined by a link are neighbours.

The adjacency matrix A

A computer stores a network as a table A with one row and one column for each node. The entry in row i, column j, written Aij, is 1 if nodes i and j are linked and 0 if not.

A=(0100110110010110110010100)

Row 1 has 1s in columns 2 and 5 because node 1 is linked to nodes 2 and 5. The table is symmetric (row i column j equals row j column i) because a link between 1 and 2 is also a link between 2 and 1.

Degree

The degree ki of node i is its number of links. Here k1=2, k2=3, k3=3, k4=2, k5=2. It is also the sum of row i of A: ki=∑jAij.

The average degree is ⟨k⟩=12/5=2.4. There is a shortcut: every link has two ends, so adding up all degrees counts each link twice, and ∑iki=2L. So ⟨k⟩=2L/N=12/5.

Degree distribution P(k)

P(k) is the fraction of nodes that have exactly k links. Here three of the five nodes have degree 2 and two have degree 3, so P(2)=3/5 and P(3)=2/5. All other values are 0. The fractions always add up to 1. It is the network version of "what fraction of the class has 2 friends, 3 friends, and so on".

Path length and diameter

The distance between two nodes is the smallest number of links you must walk along to get from one to the other. Nodes 1 and 3 are not neighbours, but 1 → 2 → 3 takes 2 steps, so their distance is 2.

The average path length ⟨l⟩ is the distance averaged over every pair of nodes. With 5 nodes there are (52)=10 pairs. Six of them are neighbours (distance 1) and the other four (1–3, 1–4, 2–5, 4–5) are at distance 2. So ⟨l⟩=(6×1+4×2)/10=14/10=1.4.

The diameter is the largest distance between any pair. Here it is 2.

Clustering coefficient

Clustering measures whether your neighbours are also neighbours of each other ("are my friends friends with each other?"). For node i with ki neighbours, there are ki(ki−1)2 possible pairs of neighbours. The clustering Ci is the fraction of those pairs that are actually linked.

Ci=number of links between neighbours of iki(ki−1)/2

Node 2 has neighbours 1, 3 and 4. The possible pairs are 1–3, 1–4, 3–4, and only 3–4 is a link. So C2=1/3. Node 4 has neighbours 2 and 3, one pair, and it is linked, so C4=1. The average over all nodes is ⟨C⟩=(0+13+13+1+0)/5=13.

Link density p

p is the fraction of all possible links that exist. With 5 nodes there are 10 possible links and 6 exist, so p=0.6. Each node could link to N−1 others and actually links to a fraction p of them, which gives ⟨k⟩=p(N−1)=0.6×4=2.4. This was a 2024 Quiz 1 question.

What to remember
  • N nodes, L links. ki = degree of node i. ⟨k⟩=2L/N.
  • P(k) = fraction of nodes with degree k.
  • ⟨l⟩ = distance averaged over all N(N−1)/2 pairs. Diameter = largest distance.
  • Ci = fraction of pairs of i's neighbours that are linked.
  • p = fraction of possible links present. ⟨k⟩=p(N−1).

Part 1Degree distributions and path length

1The power law and its averages

Syllabus · power law, ⟨k⟩, ⟨k²⟩, critical β, k_min

In a real network, like the web or a citation network, most nodes have only a few links, but a small number of nodes (called hubs) have an enormous number. A formula called a power law describes this. In this section we find the average degree of such a network, the average of the squared degrees, and the conditions under which these averages are infinite. That last part is what "critical β" and "range of β" on the syllabus mean.

What the exam can ask
  • Find the constant C in the power law, and the condition for it to exist. (2024 Quiz 2, 3 marks)
  • Find ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩, and say for which exponents they are infinite. (2024 Quiz 2, 4 marks)
  • Say how the smallest degree kmin⁡, the average degree ⟨k⟩ and the exponent are related.
  • Work out ⟨k⟩, ⟨k²⟩ and the variance from a list of degrees.

Step 0 · averages from a list of degrees

Before the power law, here is what ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩ mean on real numbers. Ten nodes have degrees

1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 5

The degree distribution is the fraction of nodes with each degree: P(1)=4/10=0.4, P(2)=0.4, P(3)=0.1, P(5)=0.1.

  • The average degree ⟨k⟩ multiplies each degree by its fraction and adds them up: ⟨k⟩=1(0.4)+2(0.4)+3(0.1)+5(0.1)=2.
  • The second moment ⟨k²⟩ does the same with each degree squared: ⟨k2⟩=1(0.4)+4(0.4)+9(0.1)+25(0.1)=5.4.
  • The variance is ⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩2=5.4−4=1.4. It measures how spread out the degrees are.

Look at the single node with degree 5. It adds only 0.5 to ⟨k⟩, but it adds 2.5 to ⟨k²⟩, almost half of the total. Squaring makes big numbers much bigger, so ⟨k²⟩ is dominated by the hubs. That is why ⟨k²⟩ is the quantity that tells you whether a network has hubs. (We reuse these ten nodes in §7.)

"Moment" is just the name for these averages. The first moment is ⟨k⟩, the average of k. The second moment is ⟨k²⟩, the average of k2.

Step 1 · what a power law is

A power law says the fraction of nodes with degree k is

P(k)=Ck−γ=Ckγ

k is a degree (number of links). P(k) is the fraction of nodes with that degree. γ (gamma) is a fixed number called the exponent. Your syllabus notes call it β and write P(k)=k−β; it is the same thing. C is a constant number that we will work out. The formula applies from some smallest degree kmin⁡ upwards (for example, every node has at least 1 link, so kmin⁡=1).

Take γ=2 and ignore C for a moment. Then P(k) is proportional to 1/k2:

Degree k12101001000
1/k210.250.010.00010.000001

Nodes with degree 1 are very common. Degree 100 is 10,000 times rarer, but it still happens. In a network with a million nodes, you expect about one node with 1000 links. That is a hub. Compare people's heights, which all sit near an average and never reach ten times the average. A power law has no "typical" size, which is why such networks are called scale-free.

Step 2 · why we use integrals ("not discrete!")

Degrees are whole numbers, so strictly we should add up P(1)+P(2)+P(3)+… with a sum. Those sums are hard to work out exactly. Instead, the course pretends k can take any value and uses an integral (the smooth version of a sum, see M7). The answers are close, and the conditions for being finite are exactly the same. This is what "not discrete!" in your notes means: treat k as continuous.

We will use one rule from M7 three times:

∫kmin⁡∞kadk=[ka+1a+1]kmin⁡∞

At the top limit (k=∞), ka+1 is 0 if the power a+1 is negative, and infinite if it is positive or zero. So:

  • if a+1<0, the answer is finite: 0−kmin⁡a+1a+1=kmin⁡a+1−(a+1);
  • if a+1≥0, the answer is infinite.

Step 3 · finding the constant C

The fractions P(k) must add up to 1 (every node has some degree). This is called normalisation. With an integral:

∫kmin⁡∞Ck−γdk=1

C is a constant, so it comes outside. The power is a=−γ, so a+1=1−γ. Using the rule:

C[k1−γ1−γ]kmin⁡∞=1

This is finite only if 1−γ<0, that is γ>1. Then the top limit gives 0, the bottom limit gives kmin⁡1−γ1−γ, and subtracting:

C⋅kmin⁡1−γγ−1=1⟹C=γ−1kmin⁡1−γ=(γ−1)kmin⁡γ−1.

(Dividing by kmin⁡1−γ is the same as multiplying by kmin⁡γ−1, by the power rules in M4.) If γ≤1, the area is infinite and no value of C can make the total equal 1.

C=(γ−1)kmin⁡γ−1,which exists only if γ>1

Step 4 · the average degree ⟨k⟩

The average is "degree times fraction, added up" (Step 0), so we integrate k×P(k):

⟨k⟩=∫kmin⁡∞k⋅Ck−γdk=C∫kmin⁡∞k1−γdk

(k×k−γ=k1−γ.) Now a=1−γ, so a+1=2−γ. This is finite only if 2−γ<0, that is γ>2. Then

⟨k⟩=C⋅kmin⁡2−γγ−2=(γ−1)kmin⁡γ−1⋅kmin⁡2−γγ−2=γ−1γ−2kmin⁡.

(The powers of kmin⁡ combine: kmin⁡γ−1×kmin⁡2−γ=kmin⁡1.)

⟨k⟩=γ−1γ−2kmin⁡,finite only if γ>2

Step 5 · the second moment ⟨k²⟩

Same again with k2×P(k), which is Ck2−γ. Now a+1=3−γ, finite only if γ>3:

⟨k2⟩=C∫kmin⁡∞k2−γdk=C⋅kmin⁡3−γγ−3=γ−1γ−3kmin⁡2.
⟨k2⟩=γ−1γ−3kmin⁡2,finite only if γ>3

Step 6 · the critical values of the exponent

Steps 3 to 5 give two critical values, 2 and 3. They split the possible exponents into three ranges.

Exponent⟨k⟩⟨k²⟩What it means
γ≤2infiniteinfiniteThe hubs are so big that even the average degree is infinite.
2<γ≤3finiteinfiniteThe usual case for real networks. The average is a normal number, but the spread of degrees has no limit.
γ>3finitefiniteHubs are rare enough that the network behaves much like a random one.

A real network has a finite number of nodes, so nothing is literally infinite. "Infinite" here means "keeps growing as the network grows". A bigger network contains bigger hubs, and because ⟨k²⟩ is dominated by the hubs, it keeps increasing with network size when γ<3.

The general pattern: the average of km is finite only if γ>m+1.

Step 7 · how k_min, ⟨k⟩ and the exponent are related

Step 4 already connects the three numbers:

⟨k⟩=γ−1γ−2kmin⁡⟺γ=2⟨k⟩−kmin⁡⟨k⟩−kmin⁡

To get the second form, multiply out ⟨k⟩(γ−2)=(γ−1)kmin⁡, collect the γ terms on one side, γ(⟨k⟩−kmin⁡)=2⟨k⟩−kmin⁡, and divide. So if you know any two of the three numbers, you can find the third.

As γ comes down towards 2, the average climbs without limit (more and bigger hubs). As γ gets large, big degrees become so rare that almost every node has the minimum degree, and ⟨k⟩ approaches kmin⁡. The average is always at least kmin⁡, as it must be.

Worked example

γ=2.5 and kmin⁡=1.

  1. C=(2.5−1)×11.5=1.5.
  2. ⟨k⟩=2.5−12.5−2×1=1.50.5=3.
  3. ⟨k²⟩ needs γ>3. Here 2.5<3, so ⟨k²⟩ is infinite.

Going backwards: if a network has kmin⁡=1 and ⟨k⟩=3, then γ=6−13−1=2.5.

For comparison · the second moment of a Poisson distribution

Your notes also ask for the second moment of the Poisson distribution, which is the degree distribution of a random network (explained in §4). For Poisson, the variance equals the average, so

⟨k2⟩=var⁡(k)+⟨k⟩2=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2,

which is always finite. For example, average degree 4 gives ⟨k2⟩=4+16=20. This is the key difference: a random network has a finite ⟨k²⟩, while a real scale-free network (2<γ<3) does not.

How to write it in the exam
  1. Write the normalisation condition ∫kmin⁡∞Ck−γdk=1. Say you treat k as continuous.
  2. Integrate. Say the value at infinity is zero only if γ>1. Get C=(γ−1)kmin⁡γ−1.
  3. For ⟨k⟩ integrate k⋅Ck−γ. Finite only if γ>2. Get γ−1γ−2kmin⁡.
  4. For ⟨k²⟩ integrate k2⋅Ck−γ. Finite only if γ>3. Get γ−1γ−3kmin⁡2.
  5. Draw the table of the three ranges and state that real networks usually have 2<γ<3: finite ⟨k⟩, infinite ⟨k²⟩.
What to remember
  • Power law: P(k)=Ck−γ (your notes write k−β).
  • C=(γ−1)kmin⁡γ−1, needs γ>1.
  • ⟨k⟩=γ−1γ−2kmin⁡, finite only for γ>2.

2Drawing a degree distribution · linear and log binning

Syllabus · binning, linear and log, log-log scale

To see a degree distribution you draw a histogram: a bar chart of how many nodes have each degree. For a power law, the ordinary way of drawing it hides the pattern. This section explains the special axes (log-log) and the special way of grouping degrees (log binning) that make a power law show up as a straight line.

What the exam can ask
  • Why plot a degree distribution on log-log axes?
  • What is linear binning, what goes wrong with it, and how does log binning fix it?
  • Given bins and counts, compute P(k), or read the exponent off a plot.

What a histogram and a bin are

Suppose you have the degrees of 1000 nodes. You split the possible degrees into ranges called bins, for example 1–5, 6–10, 11–15, and count how many nodes fall into each bin. Drawing those counts as bars gives a histogram. Linear binning means every bin has the same width. The slide steps are: find the smallest and largest value, split that range into equal intervals, put each value in its interval, and count.

For heights of people or car speeds this works well, because the values sit around a typical size. For a power law, one bin at the left holds nearly all the nodes and the rest look empty. The slides show exactly this with "linbin/lin-scale": one tall bar, then almost nothing.

What a log scale is

On a normal axis, equal distances mean equal differences: 0, 10, 20, 30. On a logarithmic (log) axis, equal distances mean equal multiples: 1, 10, 100, 1000. Each step to the right multiplies by 10. This lets one chart show both tiny and huge values. A log-log plot uses a log scale on both axes.

Why a power law is a straight line on log-log axes

Take the logarithm of both sides of P(k)=Ck−γ. Using the rules log⁡(ab)=log⁡a+log⁡b and log⁡(kn)=nlog⁡k from M5:

log⁡P(k)=log⁡C−γlog⁡k

Call y=log⁡P(k) and x=log⁡k. Then y=log⁡C−γx, which is the equation of a straight line (like y=c+mx) with slope −γ. A log-log plot draws exactly log⁡P against log⁡k. So a power law appears as a straight line, and its slope is minus the exponent. Other distributions, such as the Poisson distribution of a random network, curve sharply downwards on these axes.

To read the exponent from two points (k1,P1) and (k2,P2) on the line, use "slope = rise over run":

γ=−log⁡P2−log⁡P1log⁡k2−log⁡k1

Example: if P(10)=10−2 and P(100)=10−4.5, then in base-10 logs the rise is −4.5−(−2)=−2.5 and the run is 2−1=1, so the slope is −2.5 and γ=2.5.

What goes wrong with linear bins on log-log axes

  1. The right-hand end is noise. Large degrees are rare. With bins of equal width, most bins at large k contain 0 or 1 node. A bin with 0 cannot be drawn at all, since log⁡0 does not exist. The bins with exactly 1 node all have the same height, so they make a flat row of points (a "floor") that has nothing to do with the true distribution.
  2. The points are in the wrong places. On a log axis, equal-width bins crowd together at the right end. Most of your points land in the noisy region, and the useful left part gets only one or two points.

Log binning

In logarithmic binning, each bin is a fixed multiple wider than the previous one. For example, with a factor of 2 the bin edges are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and so on. With a factor of 10 they are 1, 10, 100, 1000. At small degrees, where there is lots of data, the bins are narrow and keep the detail. At large degrees, where data is rare, the bins are wide, so each bin still collects enough nodes to give a reliable count. On a log axis these bins are evenly spaced.

The step people forget · divide by the bin width

The bins now have different widths, and a wider bin collects more nodes just because it covers more degrees. To make them comparable, divide each count by the bin width. Also divide by the total number of nodes N to turn counts into fractions:

P(k)≈number of nodes in the binN×bin width

Plot each value at the middle of its bin. If you forget to divide by the width, the slope comes out as −(γ−1) instead of −γ.

Worked example

Bin (degrees)WidthCountCount ÷ (1000 × width)

Optional · cumulative plots

Some slide figures (word counts in Moby Dick, citations, earthquakes) are marked "these plots are cumulative". A cumulative plot shows, for each k, the fraction of nodes with degree at least k. It needs no bins at all. For a power law with exponent γ, it is also a straight line on log-log axes, with slope −(γ−1).

How to write it in the exam

  2. Linear binning uses equal-width bins. For a power law, the tail bins hold 0 or 1 node, giving noise, a flat floor, and points that cannot be plotted.
  3. Log binning makes each bin a constant factor wider (for example 2 times), so wide bins at large k collect enough nodes.
  4. Divide each count by N times the bin width, and plot at the bin centre.

3Path length and degree of star, chain and ring

Syllabus · star, chain, closed chain, ring with degree k

Here we take four simple network shapes and work out, for each, how many steps it takes on average to get from one node to another (the average path length), plus the degree of each node. The method is the same every time: count how many pairs of nodes are 1 step apart, 2 steps apart, and so on, and take the average.

What the exam can ask
  • Average path length and diameter of a star with a given number of nodes. (2024 Quiz 1: 5 nodes and 6 nodes)
  • Derive the average path length of the open chain, the ring, or the ring with degree k. Give their degrees and clustering.
  • Say how the average path length changes as the network gets bigger.

The method

  1. Count the pairs. With N nodes there are N(N−1)2 pairs (M3).
  2. Group the pairs by distance: how many are 1 step apart, how many 2 steps apart, and so on.
  3. Add up (distance × number of pairs at that distance), then divide by the number of pairs.

For the ring, every node sees the same picture around it. Then there is a shortcut: pick any one node, average its distances to the other N−1 nodes, and that is already the answer for the whole network.

Star

Start with a star of 5 nodes: one hub and 4 outer nodes (called leaves). There are 5×42=10 pairs.

  • Hub with a leaf: 4 pairs, each 1 step apart (they are directly linked).
  • Leaf with a leaf: the other 6 pairs, each 2 steps apart (leaf → hub → leaf).

Average path length =4×1+6×210=1610=1.6. The diameter (largest distance) is 2.

Now any size N. There are N−1 leaves, so N−1 hub–leaf pairs at distance 1, and (N−1)(N−2)2 leaf–leaf pairs at distance 2. Put these into the method:

⟨l⟩=(N−1)×1+(N−1)(N−2)2×2N(N−1)2=(N−1)+(N−1)(N−2)N(N−1)2

The top is (N−1) times (1+(N−2))=(N−1)2. Dividing by N(N−1)2 is the same as multiplying by 2N(N−1), and one factor of (N−1) cancels:

⟨l⟩star=2(N−1)N,diameter=2

Check with N=5: 2×4/5=1.6. For N=6: 10/6≈1.67. As N grows, 2(N−1)N=2−2N gets closer to 2 but never reaches it. The hub keeps everyone within two steps however big the star is.

Degrees: the hub has N−1 links, each leaf has 1. The average is ⟨k⟩=2LN=2(N−1)N, the same number as ⟨l⟩. Clustering is 0 everywhere: a leaf has only one neighbour, and the hub's neighbours (the leaves) are never linked to each other.

Open chain

Take 4 nodes in a line, A–B–C–D, as on the slide. The pairs at distance 1 are AB, BC, CD (3 pairs). At distance 2: AC, BD (2 pairs). At distance 3: AD (1 pair). So

⟨l⟩=3×1+2×2+1×36=106=53.

The pattern (3, 2, 1 pairs for N=4; 4, 3, 2, 1 for N=5) is that there are N−j pairs at distance j. Using the two sums from M2, the slide's calculation gives

⟨l⟩=2N(N−1)∑j=1N−1j(N−j)=2N(N−1)[N⋅N(N−1)2−(N−1)N(2N−1)6]=N−2N−13

(Inside the bracket, ∑j(N−j)=N∑j−∑j2, and each sum runs up to n=N−1.)

⟨l⟩chain=N+13,diameter=N−1

Check with N=4: 5/3. Degrees: the two end nodes have 1, the others have 2. Clustering is 0 (no triangles).

Closed chain (ring)

Join the two ends of the chain. Take 6 nodes. Stand on any one node and look both ways around the ring: 2 nodes are 1 step away, 2 nodes are 2 steps away, and 1 node (directly opposite) is 3 steps away. The average of these 5 distances is 1+1+2+2+35=95=1.8. Every node sees the same, so ⟨l⟩=1.8.

In general, a node has 2 nodes at each distance 1, 2, 3, … up to about N/2 (the node opposite). The average of the numbers from 1 to N/2 is about half of N/2, which is N/4:

⟨l⟩ring≈N4

Exact values, if asked: N+14 when N is odd, N24(N−1) when N is even. For N=6: 36/20=1.8.

Diameter is about N/2. Degree is exactly 2 for every node. The slides write this as P(k)=δ(k−2), which means "all nodes have degree 2 and no other degree occurs". Clustering is 0 (for N>3).

Ring with degree k

Now link each node to its nearest k/2 nodes on each side, so every node has degree k. The slide picture has k=4: each node is linked to the nodes 1 and 2 places away on both sides.

In one step you can now move up to k/2 places around the ring, in either direction. So each extra step reaches k new nodes (k/2 on each side). The slide writes this as 1+∑l=1lmax⁡k≈N: the starting node plus k new nodes per step, until all N are reached. That takes about

lmax⁡≈Nk steps.

Distances are spread evenly from 1 to lmax⁡, so the average is about half of lmax⁡:

⟨l⟩≈N2k,so for k=4,  ⟨l⟩≈N8

With k=2 this gives N/4, the plain ring, as it should. The slide simulation with 500 nodes found 125.25 for k=2 (formula: 500/4=125) and 62.88 for k=4 (formula: 62.5).

Degree is k for every node (P(k)=δ(k−4) on the slide). Clustering is no longer 0. For k=4, node i's neighbours are i−2, i−1, i+1, i+2. Among these 4 neighbours there are 6 possible pairs, and 3 of them are linked: (i−2,i−1), (i−1,i+1) and (i+1,i+2), since each of these is at most 2 places apart. So C=3/6=1/2 for every node, as on the slide (which says "if N>6", so that the ring is big enough for these to be different nodes).

General formula, rarely needed: C=3(k−2)4(k−1).

  • Square grid (2-D lattice): ⟨l⟩ grows like N. In D dimensions it grows like N1/D. The ring is the 1-dimensional case, growing like N.

Part 2Erdős–Rényi (ER) random networks

4Random networks · binomial degrees and the Poisson limit

Syllabus · ER 1, binomial, Poisson, ⟨k⟩, var(k)

An Erdős–Rényi (ER) network is built by chance: for every pair of nodes you flip a coin to decide whether they are linked. This section works out how many links a node ends up with. The answer is a formula called the binomial distribution, which becomes a simpler formula called the Poisson distribution when the network is large.

What the exam can ask
  • Show that the degree follows the binomial distribution, and give its average and variance.
  • Prove that it becomes Poisson when there are many nodes and the link probability is small. Give ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩. (2024 Quiz 2, 4 marks)
  • Relate the link probability p, the average degree ⟨k⟩ and the number of nodes N. (2024 Quiz 1)

How the network is built

Start with N nodes and no links. Go through every pair of nodes (there are N(N−1)2 pairs). For each pair, flip a biased coin that says "link" with probability p and "no link" with probability 1−p. Every flip is independent of the others. The result is called G(N,p).

N = number of nodes. p = probability that any given pair is linked. The slides also mention G(N,M), where you place exactly M links at random. For large networks the two behave the same.

Each node has N−1 possible partners, and each is linked with probability p. By the expected-value rule (M3, "n tries each succeeding with probability p give np on average"):

⟨k⟩=p(N−1)

Warm-up · coin flips

The slides start with this question: what is the chance of exactly 2 heads in 3 flips, if each flip is heads with probability p?

  • One way is H, H, T. By the "and" rule its probability is p×p×(1−p)=p2(1−p).
  • The other ways are H, T, H and T, H, H, each with the same probability.
  • That is (32)=3 ways (choose which 2 of the 3 flips are heads). By the "or" rule, add them: 3p2(1−p).

In general, the chance of exactly k successes in n tries is (nk)pk(1−p)n−k. This is the binomial distribution.

The degree of a node is binomial

For one node, each of its N−1 possible links is one coin flip. "Having degree k" means exactly k of those N−1 flips came up "link". So, with n=N−1:

P(k)=(N−1k)pk(1−p)N−1−k
  • (N−1k) is the number of ways to choose which k of the N−1 others are its neighbours.
  • pk is the probability that those k links are all present.
  • (1−p)N−1−k is the probability that the remaining N−1−k links are all absent.

Its average and variance are

⟨k⟩=p(N−1),var⁡(k)=σk2=p(1−p)(N−1).
Optional · where the average and variance come from

The slide does this with long sums. A shorter way: the degree is a sum of N−1 separate pieces, one per possible partner, each equal to 1 (link) with probability p or 0 (no link). One piece has average p. Its variance is ⟨x2⟩−⟨x⟩2=p−p2=p(1−p), because x2=x when x is 0 or 1. For independent pieces, averages add and variances add. So the degree has average (N−1)p and variance (N−1)p(1−p).

Why it becomes Poisson

Real networks are large and sparse: N is huge, but each node has only a few links. So p=⟨k⟩N−1 is tiny. Keep the average degree ⟨k⟩ fixed and imagine N growing very large. Three approximations simplify the binomial formula.

  1. The "choose" term. (N−1k)=(N−1)(N−2)⋯(N−k)k!. The top is k numbers multiplied together, all very close to N−1 when N is huge and k is small. So it is about (N−1)k, and (N−1k)≈(N−1)kk!.
  2. The "pk" term. Replace p by ⟨k⟩N−1. Then pk=⟨k⟩k(N−1)k.
  3. The "(1−p)N−1−k" term. Take the logarithm, which turns the power into a multiplication (M5): (N−1−k)ln⁡(1−p). For small p, ln⁡(1−p)≈−p, and since k is tiny next to N, N−1−k≈N−1. So the log is about −(N−1)p=−⟨k⟩, and the term itself is e−⟨k⟩.

Now multiply the three pieces together. The (N−1)k on top (step 1) cancels the (N−1)k underneath (step 2):

P(k)≈(N−1)kk!×⟨k⟩k(N−1)k×e−⟨k⟩
P(k)=e−⟨k⟩⟨k⟩kk!(the Poisson distribution)

It depends on only one number, the average degree ⟨k⟩. N and p no longer appear separately, which is why it is easier to use.

For the Poisson distribution, the variance equals the average: the binomial variance p(1−p)(N−1) becomes p(N−1)=⟨k⟩ when p is tiny (because 1−p≈1). Then the second moment follows from the definition of variance, var⁡=⟨k2⟩−⟨k⟩2:

⟨k⟩=var⁡(k),⟨k2⟩=var⁡(k)+⟨k⟩2=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2

What this tells you about random networks

The typical spread of degrees is σk=⟨k⟩. With an average of 100, the spread is 10, so nearly every node has between about 70 and 130 links. A node with 1000 links would be 90 spreads away from the average, which essentially never happens. So random networks have no hubs. This is the big difference from the power law of §1, where hubs are expected. Relative to the average, the spread σk/⟨k⟩=1/⟨k⟩ gets smaller as the network gets denser.

Worked example

N=1001 nodes, p=0.01. Then ⟨k⟩=0.01×1000=10.

  • Chance that a node has exactly 10 links: e−10×101010!≈0.125.
  • Chance that a node has no links: e−10≈0.000045. Expected number of isolated nodes: 1001×0.000045≈0.05, so almost certainly none.
  • Second moment: ⟨k2⟩=10+100=110.
How to write it in the exam
  1. Each of a node's N−1 possible links exists independently with probability p, so its degree is binomial. Write the formula and explain the three factors.
  2. State the limit: N→∞, p→0, with ⟨k⟩=p(N−1) fixed.
  3. Apply the three approximations: the choose term, pk, and (1−p)N−1−k≈e−⟨k⟩.
  4. Multiply, cancel (N−1)k, and box the Poisson formula.
  5. State ⟨k⟩=p(N−1), variance =⟨k⟩, and ⟨k2⟩=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2.

5The giant component and the equation for S

Syllabus · ER 2 and 3, GCC, S, critical point

If links are scarce, a random network falls apart into many small separate pieces. If you add links, at one exact point a single huge piece suddenly forms, containing a large share of all the nodes. This huge piece is the giant connected component (GCC). This section finds the equation for its size, shows that it appears exactly when the average degree passes 1, and describes how it grows just after that point.

What the exam can ask
  • Derive the self-consistent equation for S, the fraction of nodes in the giant component.
  • Prove that the giant component appears only when the average degree is greater than 1. (2024 Quiz 2, 5 marks)
  • Show how S behaves near the critical point: S∼(⟨k⟩−1)β, and find β.
  • Describe what the network looks like below, at, and above the critical point.

Words first

A component is a piece of the network in which every node can reach every other node by walking along links, and which has no links to the rest. The example in §0 is one component. A network with no links at all has N components of one node each.

Think of a class where any two students become friends with probability p. If on average each student has fewer than one friend, the class is split into many small friend groups. As the average rises past one friend, the groups rapidly join up into one big group containing a fixed share of the whole class. That big group is the giant component. Its size grows in proportion to the number of nodes, which is what "giant" means.

S = fraction of all nodes that are in the giant component (a number between 0 and 1). u=1−S = fraction of nodes that are not in it, which is also the probability that a randomly chosen node is not in it.

Deriving the equation, one step at a time

  1. Look at one node i and one other node j. For i to be outside the giant component, j must not connect i to it. That happens in two ways that cannot both be true. Either there is no link between i and j (probability 1−p), or there is a link (probability p) but j is itself outside the giant component (probability u), giving pu by the "and" rule. Add them ("or" rule): 1−p+pu.
  2. Do this for all N−1 other nodes. For i to be outside, this must hold for every one of them. They are independent, so multiply ("and" rule) N−1 copies: u=(1−p+pu)N−1=[1−p(1−u)]N−1.
  3. Make N large. Put p=⟨k⟩N−1. The bracket becomes [1−⟨k⟩(1−u)N−1]N−1. This has the form (1−xn)n with x=⟨k⟩(1−u), which approaches e−x for large n (approximation 1 in M5). So u=e−⟨k⟩(1−u).
  4. Write it in terms of S. Replace u by 1−S. Then 1−u=S, and 1−S=e−⟨k⟩S.
S=1−e−⟨k⟩S

It is called self-consistent because S appears on both sides. Whether you are in the giant component depends on whether your neighbours are in it, and the chance of that is again S. The equation cannot be rearranged into "S= something", so it is solved by drawing a graph or by repeated substitution.

Solving it with a graph · why the average degree must be above 1

Draw both sides of the equation as curves against S and look for where they meet: the straight line y=S, and the curve y=1−e−⟨k⟩S.

  1. S=0 is always a solution, since 1−e0=1−1=0. It means "no giant component".
  2. The curve starts at (0,0) with slope ⟨k⟩. (The slope of 1−e−⟨k⟩S is ⟨k⟩e−⟨k⟩S, by the rule in M6, and at S=0 that is ⟨k⟩.)
  3. The curve bends downward and never goes above 1. So if it starts less steep than the line (slope less than 1), it stays below the line and never meets it again. If it starts steeper than the line (slope more than 1), it begins above the line, and because it flattens out below 1 it must come back down and cross the line at some S between 0 and 1.
a giant component exists (S>0)⟺⟨k⟩>1⟺p>1N−1≈1N

This is the proof that "the GCC appears when the average degree is greater than 1". The borderline ⟨k⟩=1, where the curve just touches the line at the origin, is called the critical point.

Worked example · solving by repeated substitution

Take ⟨k⟩=2. Guess S=0.5, put it into the right-hand side, and repeat with the answer:

1−e−2×0.5=0.632, then 1−e−2×0.632=0.717, then 0.762, 0.782, …, settling at 0.797.

So about 80% of the nodes are in the giant component. Other values: average degree 1.5 gives S=0.58, 3 gives 0.94, 4 gives 0.98.

Behaviour at the critical point · S∼(⟨k⟩−1)β

This syllabus item asks how S grows just after the average degree passes 1. There, S is still small, so we can use approximation 2 from M5, e−x≈1−x+x22, with x=⟨k⟩S.

  1. Substitute: S=1−(1−⟨k⟩S+⟨k⟩2S22)=⟨k⟩S−⟨k⟩2S22.
  2. We want the solution where S is not 0, so divide both sides by S: 1=⟨k⟩−⟨k⟩2S2.
  3. Rearrange for S: ⟨k⟩2S2=⟨k⟩−1, so S=2(⟨k⟩−1)⟨k⟩2.
  4. Near the critical point ⟨k⟩≈1, so ⟨k⟩2≈1.
S≈2(⟨k⟩−1),soS∼(⟨k⟩−1)β  with  β=1

"∼" means "grows like" or "is proportional to". The power β here is called the critical exponent. It is 1, so S grows in a straight line just after the critical point. The slide writes the same result as NG/N∼⟨k⟩−1, where NG is the number of nodes in the giant component.

Check: for average degree 1.1, the formula gives 2×0.1/1.21=0.165, and solving the full equation gives 0.176.

This sudden change is called a phase transition, like water turning to ice at exactly 0 °C. A smooth change in one number (the average degree) causes a sudden change in structure (a giant component appears where there was none).

6Path length and clustering of random networks, compared with real ones

Syllabus · ER 4, ⟨l⟩, ⟨CC⟩, relation with real-world networks

Two questions about random networks. How far apart are two nodes on average? And how many triangles are there (do your friends know each other)? Then we compare the answers with real-world networks. Random networks get the first one right and the second one badly wrong.

What the exam can ask
  • Derive the average path length ⟨l⟩≈ln⁡N/ln⁡⟨k⟩.
  • Show that the clustering of a random network is p, and that it does not depend on a node's degree.
  • Say how each one changes with network size N, and compare with real-world networks and with regular rings.

Average path length

Stand on one node and count how many nodes you can reach in a given number of steps.

  • In 1 step you reach its neighbours: about ⟨k⟩ nodes.
  • Each of those has about ⟨k⟩ neighbours of its own, so in 2 steps you reach about ⟨k⟩×⟨k⟩=⟨k⟩2 nodes.
  • In d steps, about ⟨k⟩d nodes.

This assumes you do not meet the same node twice. In a large random network with few links per node, the area around any node looks like a tree (no loops), so this is a good approximation.

The number reached grows very fast. You run out of nodes when ⟨k⟩d reaches N. Solve ⟨k⟩d=N for d by taking logs of both sides: dln⁡⟨k⟩=ln⁡N.

⟨l⟩≈ln⁡Nln⁡⟨k⟩

The slides write the same thing more carefully: the number of nodes within distance d is 1+⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2+⋯+⟨k⟩d≈⟨k⟩d, and setting it equal to N gives the diameter dmax⁡≈ln⁡N/ln⁡⟨k⟩. The formula describes the average ⟨l⟩ even better than the diameter, because the diameter is set by a few unusual pairs.

Distance grows only like ln⁡N, which grows extremely slowly (M5). This is the small-world property: even in a huge network, everyone is a few steps from everyone else. Dividing by ln⁡⟨k⟩ means more links per node give shorter paths.

Worked example

About 8 billion people, each knowing about 1000 others: ⟨l⟩≈ln⁡(8×109)ln⁡1000=22.86.9≈3.3 steps. A ring with the same size and degree (§3) would give N/(2k)=4 million steps. This is the idea behind "six degrees of separation".

Clustering

Take a node with k neighbours. There are (k2)=k(k−1)2 pairs of neighbours. In a random network, whether a given pair of neighbours is linked is just another coin flip with probability p. Being neighbours of the same node does not make them more likely to be linked, because every link was decided independently. So on average p×(k2) of the pairs are linked, and

Ci=p(k2)(k2)=p,⟨C⟩=p=⟨k⟩N−1≈⟨k⟩N
  • This is the same for every degree k. Writing C(k) for the clustering of nodes with degree k, a random network has C(k)=p, a flat line. In real networks C(k) usually falls as k grows.
  • If the average degree stays fixed and the network grows, C=⟨k⟩/N shrinks towards 0. A large random network has almost no triangles.
Optional · the whole-network version also gives p

Another way to measure clustering, called transitivity, is 3 × (number of triangles) ÷ (number of connected triples, meaning paths of two links). The expected number of triangles is (N3)p3, since all three links must be present. The expected number of connected triples is N(N−12)p2: pick the middle node, pick two other nodes, and both links must be present. Then

T=3(N3)p3N(N−12)p2=3⋅N(N−1)(N−2)6p3N⋅(N−1)(N−2)2p2=p.

Everything cancels, and both definitions agree.

7Neighbour degree and the friendship paradox

Syllabus · ER 4, k_nn, ⟨k_nn⟩, is ⟨k_nn⟩ = ⟨k⟩? · slide 67 (starred)

On average, your friends have more friends than you do. This sounds wrong but is true in almost every network, and the reason is simple: people with many friends show up on many friend lists. This section turns that into a formula and answers the syllabus question "is the average degree of a neighbour equal to the average degree?". The answer is no, it is bigger.

What the exam can ask
  • Define knn,i (average degree of node i's neighbours) and ⟨knn⟩.
  • Show that the chance of landing on a node of degree k by following a random link is qk=kP(k)/⟨k⟩.
  • Prove that ∑kkqk=⟨k2⟩/⟨k⟩, and that for a random network this equals ⟨k⟩+1.
  • Compute pk, qk and ⟨knn⟩ for a small drawn network, as on slide 67 onwards.

The example from the slides

These are the same ten degrees as in §1, so ⟨k⟩=2 and ⟨k2⟩=5.4.

Choosing a node versus following a link

There are two ways to pick a node.

  1. Pick a node at random. Each node is equally likely. The chance of getting a node with degree k is just pk=P(k), the fraction of nodes with that degree. Here, the chance of picking the hub is p5=1/10.
  2. Pick a link at random and walk to one of its ends. Now a node with many links is more likely, because it sits at the end of many links. The hub is at the end of 5 of the 10 links, so it is reached in 5 of the 20 possible "link-end" choices: probability 5/20=1/4. That is 2.5 times more likely than picking it directly.

In general, a node of degree k is the end of k links, so it is reached k times as often. The probability of landing on some node of degree k by following a link is

qk=kP(k)⟨k⟩

qk is P(k) weighted by k. Dividing by ⟨k⟩ makes all the qk add up to 1, because ∑kkP(k)=⟨k⟩.

Is ⟨k_nn⟩ = ⟨k⟩? No

Use the definition of variance, ⟨k2⟩=⟨k⟩2+σ2 (M1), and divide by ⟨k⟩:

⟨knn⟩=⟨k⟩2+σ2⟨k⟩=⟨k⟩+σ2⟨k⟩

The extra part σ2/⟨k⟩ can never be negative. So ⟨knn⟩≥⟨k⟩, and the two are equal only when the variance is 0, meaning every node has the same degree (such as a ring). In every other network, your neighbours have more links than you on average. This is the friendship paradox. It comes from how neighbours are sampled, not from anything special about people.

For a random (ER) network, §4 gave ⟨k2⟩=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2. So

⟨knn⟩ER=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2⟨k⟩=⟨k⟩+1

Your neighbours have exactly one more link than average, whatever the average is. For a power-law network with 2<γ<3, ⟨k²⟩ is infinite (§1), so ⟨knn⟩ is huge: neighbours are far better connected than you, because many of them are hubs.

8Fixed points and stability

Syllabus · bifurcation (foundation)

A dynamical system is a rule that says how something changes over time, such as a population, the number of infected people, or the charge on a capacitor. A fixed point is a value where the change stops, so the system stays there forever. A fixed point is stable if, after a small push, the system comes back to it, and unstable if a small push sends it away. This section shows how to find fixed points and test their stability. Everything in Parts 3 and 4 uses this.

What the exam can ask
  • Find all fixed points of x˙=f(x) and say which are stable.
  • Draw the phase line (a plot of x˙ against x, with arrows).
  • Explain why a negative slope means stable.

The equation

x˙=f(x)

x is the quantity that changes (say, a population). x˙ is its rate of change over time (M6): positive means x is increasing, negative means decreasing. f(x) is a formula that gives this rate for each value of x.

Example: x˙=x(1−x). At x=0.5, the rate is 0.5×0.5=0.25, so x is growing. At x=2, the rate is 2×(−1)=−2, so x is shrinking. At x=1, the rate is 0, so x does not change.

Fixed points

A fixed point x∗ is a value where the rate of change is zero:

f(x∗)=0

For x˙=x(1−x): x(1−x)=0 when x=0 or x=1. Those are the two fixed points.

Stability, as a picture

Think of a ball on a hilly surface. A ball resting at the bottom of a valley rolls back if you nudge it: that is a stable fixed point. A ball balanced on a hilltop rolls away if you nudge it: that is an unstable fixed point. Both are places where the ball can rest, but only one survives a small disturbance.

The phase line · reading stability from a plot

Plot f(x) (the rate) against x.

  • Where the curve is above the axis, x˙>0, so x increases. Draw an arrow pointing right.
  • Where the curve is below the axis, x˙<0, so x decreases. Draw an arrow pointing left.
  • Where the curve crosses the axis, x˙=0. That is a fixed point.

If the arrows on both sides point towards a fixed point, it is stable. If they point away, it is unstable.

The slope test

Look at the picture again. At the stable point K, the curve crosses the axis going downhill (from above to below). At the unstable point 0, it crosses going uphill. Downhill means negative slope, uphill means positive slope. So you can test stability with the slope f′(x∗) (M6) instead of drawing:

f′(x∗)<0 ⇒ stable,f′(x∗)>0 ⇒ unstable,f′(x∗)=0 ⇒ the test cannot decide

The reason, in one line: if you push the system a small distance η (Greek "eta") from the fixed point, the push obeys η˙≈f′(x∗)η. That is the equation y˙=ay from M8 with a=f′(x∗), so the push shrinks if the slope is negative and grows if it is positive.

Optional · the full reasoning

Write x=x∗+η, where η is small. Since x∗ does not change, x˙=η˙. Near x∗, the curve f is close to its tangent line, so f(x∗+η)≈f(x∗)+f′(x∗)η. The first term is 0 because x∗ is a fixed point. So η˙≈f′(x∗)η, whose solution is η(t)=η(0)ef′(x∗)t. When the slope is exactly 0 the tangent line is flat and says nothing, which is why the test fails there. Those are exactly the places where bifurcations happen (§9).

Worked example · logistic growth

x˙=rx(1−xK), with r and K positive numbers. (x is a population, r its growth rate, K the largest population the environment can support.)

  1. Fixed points: rx(1−x/K)=0 when x=0 or x=K.
  2. Multiply out f(x)=rx−rKx2, so the slope is f′(x)=r−2rKx.
  3. f′(0)=r>0: unstable. f′(K)=r−2r=−r<0: stable.

So any small positive population grows until it reaches K and then stays there.

How to write it in the exam
  1. Set f(x)=0 and solve for every fixed point.
  2. Differentiate to get f′(x).
  3. Put each fixed point into f′: negative means stable, positive means unstable.
  4. If asked, draw the phase line with arrows to confirm.
What to remember
  • Fixed point: f(x∗)=0.
  • f′(x∗)<0 stable, f′(x∗)>0 unstable, f′(x∗)=0 undecided.
  • Phase line: arrows towards the point means stable.

9The four bifurcations

Syllabus · bifurcation

Most systems contain a number you can adjust, called a parameter (for example a growth rate or an infection rate), written r. As you slowly change r, the fixed points move. Usually nothing dramatic happens. But at certain values of r, fixed points suddenly appear, disappear, or swap between stable and unstable. That sudden change is a bifurcation. There are four standard types in one dimension, and each has a simple example equation called its normal form.

What the exam can ask
  • "Describe the role of r. What type of bifurcation do you expect?" for a given equation. (2024 mid-sem Q3, 7 marks)
  • Draw the bifurcation diagram (fixed points against r), marking stable and unstable branches.
  • State the normal form of each bifurcation.

Watching one bifurcation happen

Take x˙=r−x2 and try three values of r.

  • r=−1: −1−x2=0 means x2=−1, which has no real solution. No fixed points.
  • r=0: −x2=0 gives only x=0. One fixed point.
  • r=1: 1−x2=0 gives x=1 and x=−1. Two fixed points. The slope is f′(x)=−2x, so f′(1)=−2 (stable) and f′(−1)=2 (unstable).

As r passes 0, a stable and an unstable fixed point appear together out of nothing. That is a saddle-node bifurcation. For general r>0 the fixed points are x∗=±r.

A bifurcation diagram draws this: r along the horizontal axis, the fixed points x∗ up the vertical axis, with each branch marked stable or unstable.

Line style used by the course

The course note draws stable branches dashed and unstable branches solid. Most textbooks do the opposite. The diagrams below follow the course note and also use colour: teal = stable, orange = unstable. In the exam, write the word "stable" or "unstable" next to each branch so there is no confusion.

The four types, worked out

For each one: set f(x)=0 to find the fixed points, then use the slope to test them, exactly as in §8.

Saddle-node · x˙=r−x2

Fixed points x∗=±r, which exist only for r≥0. Slope f′=−2x: at +r it is negative (stable), at −r positive (unstable). What happens: a stable/unstable pair is created from nothing (or, going the other way, collides and disappears).

Transcritical · x˙=rx−x2=x(r−x)

Fixed points x∗=0 and x∗=r, for every r. Slope f′=r−2x: at 0 it is r, at r it is r−2r=−r. So for r<0, 0 is stable and r is unstable; for r>0, 0 is unstable and r is stable. What happens: the two branches cross and exchange stability.

Supercritical pitchfork · x˙=rx−x3=x(r−x2)

Fixed points x∗=0 always, plus ±r when r>0. Slope f′=r−3x2: at 0 it is r; at ±r it is r−3r=−2r, negative. What happens: 0 loses stability at r=0 and two stable branches grow out of it, like the prongs of a pitchfork.

Subcritical pitchfork · x˙=rx+x3=x(r+x2)

Fixed points x∗=0 always, plus ±−r when r<0. Slope f′=r+3x2: at 0 it is r; at ±−r it is r−3r=−2r, which is positive because r<0. What happens: the two side branches are unstable. After r=0 nothing nearby is stable, so a small push sends the system far away. This is linked to sudden, dramatic jumps.

How to tell which type you have

  1. Solve f(x;r)=0 for the fixed points in terms of r, and note for which r they exist.
  2. Count the fixed points on each side of the critical value of r.
  3. Find the slope f′ on each branch and mark it stable or unstable.
  4. Match the pattern.
    • 0 fixed points on one side and 2 on the other: saddle-node.
    • 2 on both sides, crossing and swapping stability: transcritical.
    • 1 becomes 3, symmetric about 0: pitchfork. Supercritical if the new branches are stable, subcritical if unstable.

Bifurcations happen only where the slope test fails, so the critical point can be found by solving f=0 and f′=0 together.

Worked example · 2024 mid-sem Q3(a)

x˙=rx−x2, where r is a parameter. Describe the role of r. What type of bifurcation do you expect?

  1. x(r−x)=0, so the fixed points are x∗=0 and x∗=r.
  2. The slope is f′(x)=r−2x. So f′(0)=r and f′(r)=−r.
  3. For r<0: f′(0)<0, so 0 is stable, and x∗=r is unstable. For r>0: 0 is unstable and x∗=r is stable.
  4. The two branches meet at r=0 and exchange stability: a transcritical bifurcation.

Role of r: it acts like a growth rate. When r is negative, any small x dies out to 0. When r is positive, 0 becomes unstable and x settles at x∗=r.

Worked example · 2024 mid-sem Q3(b)

10Bistability and hysteresis · the spruce budworm

Syllabus · bifurcation (course note example)

Some systems have two different stable states for the same value of the parameter. Which one you end up in depends on where you start. This is bistability. If you then slowly raise the parameter and lower it again, the system jumps between the two states at two different points, so the way up is not the way down. That is hysteresis. The course note shows both with a model of an insect pest, the spruce budworm.

What the exam can ask
  • Explain bistability and the role of the unstable middle fixed point.
  • Explain hysteresis using the budworm model.
  • State how to find a fold point: solve f=0 and f′=0 together.

The model

x˙=rx(1−xK)−x21+x2,K=10

x is the budworm population. The first part is logistic growth (§8) with growth rate r. The second part is birds eating budworms. It levels off for large x, because birds can only eat so many however many budworms there are.

x∗=0 is always a fixed point, and it is unstable, since the slope there is r>0. Dividing the rest by x and clearing the fractions, the other fixed points solve a cubic (an equation with x3):

−r10x3+rx2−(1+r10)x+r=0.

A cubic can have up to three solutions. That is why up to three positive fixed points can exist at the same time.

rPositive fixed pointsStability
0.250.26stable (only a low state)
0.380.43stable (just below the first fold at 0.384)
0.500.68, 2.00, 7.32stable, unstable, stable
0.567.73stable (only a high state, just past the second fold at 0.560)

Bistability

For r between about 0.384 and 0.560, there are three positive fixed points: stable, unstable, stable. The low stable state is a small "refuge" population kept down by birds. The high stable state is an "outbreak". The unstable one in the middle is a threshold: at r=0.5, a population starting below 2 ends at 0.68, and one starting above 2 ends at 7.32.

Hysteresis

  1. Start with a small r, on the low branch, and slowly increase r. The population stays low.
  2. At r≈0.560 the low stable state meets the unstable middle state and both disappear (a saddle-node, §9). The population has nowhere nearby to settle, so it jumps up to the outbreak branch.
  3. Now slowly decrease r. The population stays on the high branch, even below 0.560, until r≈0.384, where the high state disappears in the other fold. Only then does it drop back down.

The path going up differs from the path coming down. That is hysteresis. In practice, it means that undoing the cause of an outbreak (bringing r back to where it was) does not end the outbreak. You have to push r much lower.

Finding a fold

At a fold, the curve of x˙ just touches the axis without crossing it. Two things are true at once there: it is a fixed point, and the slope is zero.

f(x∗;r)=0andf′(x∗;r)=0

Solving these two equations together gives the fold points (for the budworm with K=10, r≈0.384 and r≈0.560).

What to remember
  • Bistable: two stable states with an unstable threshold between them.
  • Hysteresis: the jump up and the jump down happen at different r. Each jump is a saddle-node (fold).
  • Fold: f=0 and f′=0 together. Budworm folds at r≈0.384 and 0.560.

11Disease models · SIS and SIR

Syllabus · network dynamics (epidemic models)

These models track what fraction of a population is infected over time. In SIS, people who recover can catch the disease again (like the flu or some infections). In SIR, people who recover are immune and never catch it again. For SIS we solve the equation completely, find the level the infection settles at, and find the condition for the disease to die out. This was a full 7-mark question in the 2024 mid-sem.

What the exam can ask
  • Solve the SIS model for I(t) in terms of β, γ and time. (2024 mid-sem Q4a, 4 marks)
  • Find the endemic equilibrium, the value of I as t→∞. (Q4b, 1.5 marks)
  • Explain when the disease does not spread. (Q4c, 1.5 marks)
  • Short questions on SIR.

Setting up SIS

Split everyone into two groups, susceptible (healthy, can catch it) and infected. Write them as fractions of the population:

S = fraction susceptible, I = fraction infected, with S+I=1. β (beta) = infection rate: how fast contact between a susceptible and an infected person causes new infections. γ (gamma) = recovery rate: the fraction of infected people who recover per unit of time.

Two things change I.

  • New infections need a susceptible person to meet an infected one. With everyone mixing equally ("homogeneous mixing"), meetings happen in proportion to S×I, so infections happen at rate βSI.
  • Recoveries happen at rate γI. Recovered people become susceptible again.
dIdt=βSI−γI

Since S=1−I, replace S:

dIdt=βI(1−I)−γI=(β−γ)I−βI2.

Give two combinations names, to make the algebra shorter: a=β−γ and K=1−γβ. You can check that aI(1−IK)=aI−aKI2, and aK=β−γ(β−γ)/β=β. So the equation becomes

dIdt=aI(1−IK),a=β−γ,K=1−γβ

This is exactly the logistic equation of §8, with growth rate a and ceiling K.

Solving it, step by step

The method is called separation of variables: move everything with I to one side and everything with t to the other, then integrate both sides.

  1. Separate. Divide both sides by I(1−I/K) and multiply by dt: dII(1−I/K)=adt.
  2. Split the fraction into two simpler ones (this trick is called partial fractions): 1I(1−I/K)=1I+1/K1−I/K. You can check it by adding the two fractions on the right back together.
  3. Integrate both sides. The integral of 1I is ln⁡I. The integral of 1/K1−I/K is −ln⁡(1−IK). The integral of a with respect to t is at. Adding a constant c: ln⁡I−ln⁡(1−IK)=at+c.
  4. Remove the logs. ln⁡A−ln⁡B=ln⁡AB, and applying e to both sides undoes ln⁡: I1−I/K=Aeat,A=ec. At t=0, I=I0 (the starting fraction infected), so A=I01−I0/K.
  5. Solve for I. Rearranging gives I=K1+KAe−at, and KA=K−I0I0.
I(t)=K1+K−I0I0e−(β−γ)t,K=1−γβ

Check at t=0: e0=1, so I=K1+(K−I0)/I0=KI0I0+K−I0=I0. Correct.

The long run and the threshold

Everything depends on the sign of a=β−γ, because of the term e−at (M5).

  • If β>γ (people infect faster than they recover), a>0 and e−at shrinks to 0. Then I(t)→K. This long-run level is the endemic equilibrium: I∗=1−γβ=1−1R0,R0=βγ. R0 is called the basic reproduction number.
  • If β<γ (people recover faster than they infect), a<0 and e−at grows without limit, so the bottom of the fraction becomes huge and I(t)→0. The disease dies out.
  • If β=γ, the equation is I˙=−βI2, which also shrinks to 0, slowly.

So the disease does not spread when β<γ, that is R0<1.

The slope test of §8 gives the same answer. With f(I)=(β−γ)I−βI2, the slope at the disease-free state I=0 is f′(0)=β−γ. That is negative (stable, the disease cannot take hold) exactly when β<γ. As β/γ passes 1, the fixed points I∗=0 and I∗=1−γ/β cross and swap stability, which is a transcritical bifurcation (§9).

How to write it in the exam
  1. Put S=1−I to get (β−γ)I−βI2. Name a=β−γ and K=1−γ/β, giving the logistic form.
  2. Separate variables, split with partial fractions, integrate, and use I(0)=I0. Box I(t).
  3. As t→∞: I∗=1−γ/β when β>γ.
  4. No spread when β<γ (R0<1), because then I(t)→0. Support it with f′(0)=β−γ<0.

SIR

In SIR, recovered people move to a third group, removed (R), meaning immune or dead, and never return. With fractions s, i, r adding to 1:

dsdt=−βsi,didt=βsi−γi,drdt=γi.
  • Assumptions: everyone mixes equally, and the recovery rate γ is constant, meaning everyone is infectious for the same length of time on average.
  • s can only go down, because nothing flows back into the susceptible group.
  • The three rates always add to zero (−βsi+βsi−γi+γi=0), because the total s+i+r stays 1.
  • At the start almost everyone is susceptible (s≈1), so i˙≈(β−γ)i. An outbreak takes off only if R0=β/γ>1. Unlike SIS, it always ends with i→0, because the susceptibles run out.
Optional · SIR-X, the quarantine model on the slides

The COVID model of Maier and Brockmann adds a quarantined group X. Containment measures remove susceptibles at rate κ0 (kappa), and infected people are also isolated at rate κ:

S˙=−βSI−κ0S,I˙=βSI−γI−κ0I−κI,R˙=γI+κ0S,X˙=(κ+κ0)I.

The extra terms drain people out of S and I, which slows the growth of infections.

What to remember
  • SIS: I˙=βI(1−I)−γI, logistic with a=β−γ, K=1−γ/β.
  • I(t)=K/(1+K−I0I0e−(β−γ)t).
  • I∗=1−γ/β. Dies out if β<γ (R0=β/γ<1).
  • SIR: s only falls; rates add to zero; outbreak needs R0>1.

12The graph Laplacian

Syllabus · network dynamics (Module 2c2 slides)

The Laplacian is another table of numbers that describes a network, built from the degrees and the adjacency matrix. It is the tool that describes how things spread and even out across a network (§13). Its eigenvalues (M9) also tell you how many separate pieces the network has.

What the exam can ask
  • Write down the Laplacian of a given network.
  • Show that 0 is always one of its eigenvalues.
  • Count the separate pieces of a network from its Laplacian, and explain the "algebraic connectivity".

Definition

Start from the adjacency matrix A (§0). Make a second table D with the degrees down the diagonal and zeros elsewhere. The Laplacian is L=D−A. Entry by entry:

Lij={kiif i=j (on the diagonal: the degree)−1if i≠j and i,j are linked0otherwise

The slides also write this as Lij=δijki−Aij. The symbol δij (the "Kronecker delta") is 1 when i=j and 0 otherwise, so it just puts ki on the diagonal.

Worked example · the network from §0

Links 1–2, 1–5, 2–3, 2–4, 3–4, 3–5. Degrees 2, 3, 3, 2, 2. Put the degrees on the diagonal and −1 wherever A has a 1:

L=(2−100−1−13−1−100−13−1−10−1−120−10−102)

Notice that every row adds up to 0. Its eigenvalues (worked out by computer) are 0, 1.38, 2.38, 3.62, 4.62.

Its properties, one at a time

  1. It is symmetric for an undirected network (row i column j equals row j column i). So its eigenvalues are real numbers and its eigenvectors are at right angles to each other (M9).
  2. Every row adds to zero. Row i has ki on the diagonal and one −1 for each of its ki neighbours. So multiplying L by the vector of all ones, 𝟏=(1,1,…,1), adds up each row and gives zero: L𝟏=(k1−∑jA1j⋮ki−∑jAij⋮)=(0⋮0⋮)=0×𝟏. So 𝟏 is always an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0.
  3. No eigenvalue is negative. So 0 is the smallest one. They are numbered from smallest to largest, 0=λ1≤λ2≤⋯≤λN.
  4. The number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of separate pieces (components). If a network is in c separate pieces, then for each piece, the vector that is 1 on that piece and 0 everywhere else also has all its row sums zero. That gives c different eigenvectors with eigenvalue 0. The number of zero eigenvalues is also called the nullity of L.
  5. Algebraic connectivity. The second-smallest eigenvalue λ2 is greater than 0 exactly when the network is in one piece (connected). It is called the algebraic connectivity. A larger λ2 means the network is harder to cut into two parts, and (§13) that things spread and even out across it faster.

In the example, there is exactly one zero eigenvalue, so the network is in one piece, and its algebraic connectivity is λ2=1.38.

Optional · why no eigenvalue can be negative

For any vector 𝐱, the product 𝐱TL𝐱 works out to ∑links (i,j)(xi−xj)2, a sum of squares, which cannot be negative. For an eigenvector of length 1, this product equals its eigenvalue. So every eigenvalue is at least 0. It equals 0 only if xi=xj across every link, meaning 𝐱 is constant on each piece, which is also why the zero eigenvalues match the pieces.

What to remember
  • L=D−A: degrees on the diagonal, −1 for each link.
  • Rows add to 0, so L𝟏=0: eigenvalue 0 always.
  • All eigenvalues ≥0. Number of zero eigenvalues (nullity) = number of components.
  • λ2>0 exactly when connected. λ2 = algebraic connectivity.

13Dynamics on networks · diffusion and infection

Syllabus · network dynamics (Module 2c2 slides, 2024 mid-sem Q5)

Now each node of a network has its own value that changes over time, and neighbours affect each other through the links. Two examples from the slides. In diffusion, something (heat, water, money) flows along links from fuller nodes to emptier ones until everything evens out. In the SI model on a network, an infection passes along links. For both, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors from M9 and §12 tell us what happens.

What the exam can ask
  • Derive the diffusion equation 𝐱˙=−CL𝐱 and solve it.
  • Simplify the network SI or SIS model for early times and find how fast the infection grows.
  • "Explain how the network affects the infection at the initial stage, and in which case the disease will not spread." (2024 mid-sem Q5, 7 marks)

The general form

x˙i=fi(xi)+∑jAijgij(xi,xj)

xi is the value at node i. fi says how node i would change on its own, with no links. The sum adds the effect of each neighbour j, given by the function gij. The Aij (1 for neighbours, 0 otherwise) makes sure only neighbours count.

Diffusion

Picture water tanks connected by pipes. Water flows from the fuller tank to the emptier one, faster when the difference in levels is bigger. Eventually all tanks have the same level. On a network, node i holds an amount xi, and along each link the amount flows at a rate C times the difference.

  1. The flow from neighbour j into i is C(xj−xi). If j has more, this is positive, so i gains.
  2. Add over all neighbours: x˙i=C∑jAij(xj−xi).
  3. Split the sum into two parts: x˙i=C∑jAijxj−Cxi∑jAij. The second sum is just the degree ki (§0). So x˙i=C∑jAijxj−Ckixi=C∑j(Aij−δijki)xj.
  4. The bracket is −Lij (§12). For all nodes at once, in matrix form:
𝐱˙=C(A−D)𝐱=−CL𝐱

Solving it with eigenvectors

This is N linked equations, one per node. The trick from M9 is to describe 𝐱 as a mix of the eigenvectors 𝐯r of L, with amounts ar(t) that change over time:

𝐱(t)=∑rar(t)𝐯r.

Put this into the equation and use L𝐯r=λr𝐯r:

∑rdardt𝐯r=−C∑rar(t)λr𝐯r.

Because the eigenvectors are at right angles to each other, each one can be matched separately on the two sides. (Formally, multiply both sides by one eigenvector 𝐯s; since 𝐯r⋅𝐯s=0 when r≠s and 1 when r=s, only one term survives.) That leaves, for each r, the single equation of M8:

dardt=−Cλrar⟹ar(t)=ar(0)e−Cλrt
  • For λ1=0, e0=1, so that part never changes. Its eigenvector is the all-ones vector (§12).
  • For every other eigenvalue (all positive if the network is connected), e−Cλrt shrinks to 0.

So after a long time only the all-ones part remains, and every node ends up with the same value. The total ∑ixi never changes (whatever leaves one node enters another), so that common value is the average of the starting values. The last part to fade is the one with λ2, so the algebraic connectivity sets how quickly the network evens out: roughly in a time 1/(Cλ2).

SI on a network

Now xi is the probability that node i is infected, and 1−xi the probability it is still healthy. A healthy node catches the infection from each infected neighbour at rate β. Nobody recovers (that is what SI means):

x˙i=β(1−xi)∑jAijxj

∑jAijxj adds up the infection of node i's neighbours. (1−xi) is the chance that i is still healthy and can be infected. The slides also write the same model for the healthy fraction, s˙i=−βsi∑jAij(1−sj) with si+xi=1.

These equations cannot be solved exactly for a general network. The slides look at the start of the epidemic instead.

  1. At the start, only one node or a few nodes are infected, so every xi is very small.
  2. Multiply out β(1−xi)∑jAijxj=β∑jAijxj−βxi∑jAijxj. The second part is a small number times a small number, which is tiny, so drop it. Then x˙i≈β∑jAijxj, or in matrix form 𝐱˙=βA𝐱.
  3. Use the eigenvectors 𝐯r of A, with eigenvalues κr (kappa), exactly as for diffusion. Each part grows as ar(t)=ar(0)eβκrt.
  4. The part with the largest eigenvalue κ1 grows fastest and soon dominates all the others.
𝐱(t)≈∑rar(0)eβκrt𝐯r ∼ eβκ1t𝐯1

What this means, as the slides say:

  • The number infected grows exponentially at first, as in a fully mixed population, but the growth rate is βκ1. It depends on the network through the largest eigenvalue κ1 of the adjacency matrix.
  • Early on, the chance that node i is infected is roughly proportional to the i-th entry of the leading eigenvector 𝐯1. Well-connected nodes, and nodes next to well-connected nodes, are infected first.
Worked example

For the five-node network, κ1=2.48 and 𝐯1=(0.36, 0.53, 0.53, 0.43, 0.36). An infection grows like e2.48βt at first. Nodes 2 and 3, which have 3 links each, have the largest entries, so they are most at risk early on.

SIS on a network · when does the disease spread?

Now add recovery at rate γ. This is the model in 2024 mid-sem Q5:

x˙i=−γxi+β(1−xi)∑jAijxj.

At early times, drop the small-times-small term as before: 𝐱˙=βA𝐱−γ𝐱. The eigenvectors are still those of A, and each part now grows at rate βκr−γ. The fastest part grows only if βκ1−γ>0:

the disease spreads if  βγ>1κ1,and dies out if  βγ<1κ1

Compare this with §11, where everyone mixes equally and the condition was β/γ>1. On a network the threshold is 1/κ1. A denser network, or one with hubs, has a larger κ1, so its threshold is lower and diseases spread more easily. (When every node has degree k, κ1=k.) For the five-node example, the disease spreads if β/γ>1/2.48≈0.40.

How to write it in the exam · 2024 mid-sem Q5
  1. (a) At t≈0 every ik is small, so drop the ikij term: 𝐢˙=(βA−γI)𝐢, where I here is the identity matrix (1s on the diagonal).
  2. Write 𝐢 in terms of the eigenvectors of A. Each part grows like e(βκr−γ)t, and the largest eigenvalue κ1 dominates, so 𝐢(t)∼e(βκ1−γ)t𝐯1.
  3. So the network affects the early stage through κ1 (how fast it grows) and 𝐯1 (which nodes are infected first).
  4. (b) The disease does not spread when βκ1−γ<0, that is β/γ<1/κ1.
What to remember
  • General: x˙i=fi(xi)+∑jAijgij(xi,xj).
  • Diffusion: 𝐱˙=−CL𝐱; ar(t)=ar(0)e−Cλrt; all nodes end at the average; speed set by λ2.
  • SI early: 𝐱˙=βA𝐱; 𝐱∼eβκ1t𝐯1.
  • SIS on a network: spreads if β/γ>1/κ1.

Mid Q5Network SIS: i˙k=−γik+β(1−ik)∑jAkjij. (a) How does the network affect the infection at the initial stage? (b) When will the disease not spread? [3.5 + 3.5]›

Mid Q2z˙=(μ+iω−|z|2)z, z=x+iy. (a) Transform into the (r,θ) plane. (b) Find when there is a stable limit cycle, with bifurcation analysis. [3 + 4]›

Limit cycles were not in this year's slides. The question is included because it reduces to the pitchfork of §9.

(a) Write z=reiθ. Then z˙=r˙eiθ+irθ˙eiθ, and the right side is (μ+iω−r2)reiθ. Cancel eiθ and match real and imaginary parts:

r˙=μr−r3,θ˙=ω.

(b) The angle just turns at constant speed ω. The radius obeys the supercritical pitchfork normal form. For μ<0, r∗=0 is the only fixed point and it is stable (f′(0)=μ<0), so trajectories spiral into the origin. For μ>0, r∗=0 becomes unstable and r∗=μ appears with f′(μ)=−2μ<0, stable. A circle of radius μ that attracts nearby trajectories is a stable limit cycle. So the condition is μ>0. The change at μ=0 is called a supercritical Hopf bifurcation.

Mid Q1Two coupled phase oscillators θ˙1=ω1+k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1), θ˙2=ω2+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2). (a) Critical coupling for a common frequency. (b) The common frequency; is it possible for k1=k2=2, ω1=2, ω2=10? [3 + 4]›

Coupled oscillators were not in this year's slides. The method is just the fixed-point analysis of §8, applied to the phase difference.

(a) Let ϕ=θ2−θ1. Subtract the equations: ϕ˙=(ω2−ω1)−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ. A common frequency means ϕ stops changing, a fixed point: sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−ω1k1+k2. This has a solution only if |ω2−ω1|≤k1+k2, so the critical coupling is k1+k2=|ω2−ω1| (for equal couplings, kc=|ω2−ω1|/2). The stable fixed point is the one with cos⁡ϕ∗>0, since dϕ˙dϕ=−(k1+k2)cos⁡ϕ.

(b) At lock θ˙1=θ˙2=Ω. From Ω=ω1+k1sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−k2sin⁡ϕ∗, eliminate sin⁡ϕ∗: Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2. With k=2, ω1=2, ω2=10: |Δω|=8>k1+k2=4, so there is no fixed point and no common frequency. The phase difference keeps drifting. (If they could lock, Ω would be 6.)

SurpriseAdjacency matrix with rows A: 0 1 1 0 0, B: 1 0 0 0 1, C: 1 0 0 1 1, D: 0 0 1 0 0, E: 0 1 1 0 0. How many connected components? What is the nullity of the Laplacian?›

The rule is that the nullity of the Laplacian (number of zero eigenvalues) equals the number of components. As printed, the links are A–B, A–C, B–E, C–D, C–E, which form one connected graph, so the answer would be 1 component and nullity 1. The official key says 3 components, {A, B, C}, {D}, {E}, and nullity 3, which only fits if D and E had no links. Either way, the answer to the second part equals the answer to the first.

SurpriseSIR: What is assumed about the infectious period? What happens to the number of susceptibles over time?›

The infectious period is the same for all individuals (constant recovery rate γ). The susceptible number decreases over time, since nobody returns to S (roughly exponentially during the epidemic).
