MThe new maths you need

This half of the course uses a few tools that the first half did not: the determinant and eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix, complex numbers, partial derivatives and iterated maps. Each is explained here with an example. Sums, integrals, e, ln and ordinary slopes are explained in section M of the Mid-sem guide.

M1 · Trace and determinant of a 2×2 matrix

For a 2×2 matrix A=(abcd):

  • the trace is the sum of the diagonal, τ=a+d (τ is Greek "tau");
  • the determinant is "down-diagonal minus up-diagonal", Δ=ad−bc (Δ is Greek "delta").

Example: A=(114−2) has τ=1+(−2)=−1 and Δ=1×(−2)−1×4=−6.

M2 · Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix, step by step

Recall (Mid-sem guide M9): an eigenvector 𝐯 is a direction that the matrix only stretches, A𝐯=λ𝐯, and the stretch λ is the eigenvalue. To find them:

  1. Eigenvalues. Solve the quadratic λ2−τλ+Δ=0. By the quadratic formula, λ=τ±τ2−4Δ2. (Why this equation: A𝐯=λ𝐯 means (A−λI)𝐯=0 for a non-zero 𝐯, which happens only when det⁡(A−λI)=0. Multiplying out (a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=0 gives exactly this quadratic.)
  2. Eigenvector for each λ. Write (A−λI)𝐯=0, meaning subtract λ from both diagonal entries and multiply by 𝐯=(v1,v2). The two rows give the same condition; use one of them to find the ratio v2/v1. Any multiple of an eigenvector is also an eigenvector, so pick a simple one.
Worked example

A=(114−2), with τ=−1, Δ=−6.

  1. λ2+λ−6=0, which factorises as (λ−2)(λ+3)=0. So λ1=2, λ2=−3.
  2. For λ=2: A−2I=(−114−4). The first row says −v1+v2=0, so v2=v1. Take 𝐯1=(1,1).
  3. For λ=−3: A+3I=(4141). The first row says 4v1+v2=0, so v2=−4v1. Take 𝐯2=(1,−4).
  4. Check: A(1,1)=(1+1, 4−2)=(2,2)=2×(1,1). Correct.

Two shortcuts: the eigenvalues always add up to the trace (2+(−3)=−1) and multiply to the determinant (2×(−3)=−6). For a triangular matrix (zeros above or below the diagonal), the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries.

M3 · Complex numbers

Some quadratics have no real solution, for example λ2+1=0. We write i for a number with i2=−1, so the solutions are λ=±i. A complex number is a+ib, with real part a and imaginary part b. When τ2−4Δ<0, the eigenvalues are a pair λ=τ2±i4Δ−τ22.

The key fact is Euler's formula: eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ. So

e(a+ib)t=eat(cos⁡bt+isin⁡bt).

This is what a complex eigenvalue does to a solution. The real part a decides growth (a>0) or decay (a<0), and the imaginary part b makes it rotate (oscillate) at angular speed b. If a=0, it just rotates forever.

Polar form. A complex number z=x+iy can be described by its distance r from 0 and its angle θ: z=reiθ, with x=rcos⁡θ, y=rsin⁡θ, r=|z|=x2+y2. Multiplying by eiθ rotates; multiplying by a positive number stretches.

Rate of change in polar form (by the product rule for slopes): if z=reiθ with r and θ changing in time, then

z˙=r˙eiθ+riθ˙eiθ=(r˙+irθ˙)eiθ.

M4 · Partial derivatives

For a formula with two variables, f(x,y), the partial derivative ∂f∂x is the slope in the x direction: differentiate as usual, treating y as a fixed number. Example with f=3x−x2−2xy:

  • ∂f∂x=3−2x−2y (the −2xy term behaves like −2y×x, slope −2y);
  • ∂f∂y=−2x (only the −2xy term contains y).

M5 · Maps (step-by-step systems)

A differential equation x˙=f(x) changes smoothly in time. A map xn+1=f(xn) jumps in steps: given this year's value xn, the formula gives next year's xn+1. Example: xn+1=2xn starting from x0=1 gives 1, 2, 4, 8, … A fixed point of a map is a value that maps to itself, x∗=f(x∗).

What to remember
  • τ=a+d, Δ=ad−bc. Eigenvalues λ=τ±τ2−4Δ2. Eigenvectors from one row of (A−λI)𝐯=0.
  • e(a+ib)t=eat(cos⁡bt+isin⁡bt): real part = grow or decay, imaginary part = rotate.
  • z=reiθ, z˙=(r˙+irθ˙)eiθ.

Part 12-D linear systems

1Two variables at once · straight-line solutions

Deck 11 · slides 4–15

In the first half, one quantity x changed over time. Now two quantities change together, x and y, and each one's rate depends on both. The simplest case is a linear system, where each rate is just a weighted sum of x and y. Such a system can be solved exactly, and the solution is built from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix.

What the exam can ask
  • Show that 𝐱(t)=eλt𝐯 solves 𝐱˙=A𝐱 when 𝐯 is an eigenvector, and derive λ2−τλ+Δ=0.
  • Write the general solution.
  • For x˙=ax, y˙=−y, describe what happens for different values of a (node, star, line of fixed points, saddle).

The system

x˙=ax+by,y˙=cx+dy⟺𝐱˙=A𝐱,A=(abcd),𝐱=(xy).

a,b,c,d are fixed numbers. 𝐱 is the point (x,y). The plane of all possible points is called the phase plane, and a drawing of how points move in it is a phase portrait. The origin (0,0) is always a fixed point, because there both rates are 0.

A first example with no coupling

Take b=c=0 and d=−1: x˙=ax, y˙=−y. Each equation involves only one variable, so each is the basic equation y˙=ay from the maths primer (Mid-sem M8):

x(t)=x0eat,y(t)=y0e−t.

y always shrinks. What x does depends on a, and that gives five pictures, all on the slides.

In the node case, which direction is "slower"? For a<−1, x shrinks faster than y (rate |a|>1), so the x-part disappears first and paths come into the origin along the y-axis. For −1<a<0 it is the other way round: they come in along the x-axis.

The general case · looking for straight-line solutions

In the uncoupled example, a point on an axis stays on that axis and just slides in or out. Those are straight-line solutions. The idea for any matrix A is to find the straight lines that the motion keeps, which turn out to be the eigenvectors.

Derivation to learn · eigen-solutions and the characteristic equation
  1. Guess a solution that stays on one line and grows or shrinks exponentially: 𝐱(t)=eλt𝐯, where 𝐯 is a fixed vector and λ a fixed number.
  2. Put it into the equation. Left side: 𝐱˙=λeλt𝐯 (slope of eλt is λeλt). Right side: A𝐱=eλtA𝐯. So λeλt𝐯=eλtA𝐯.
  3. Cancel eλt, which is never zero: A𝐯=λ𝐯. So the guess works exactly when 𝐯 is an eigenvector of A and λ is its eigenvalue. This is called an eigen-solution.
  4. Find λ. A𝐯=λ𝐯 with 𝐯≠0 needs det⁡(A−λI)=0: det⁡(a−λbcd−λ)=(a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=0.
  5. Multiply out: λ2−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc)=0, that is λ2−τλ+Δ=0,τ=a+d,Δ=ad−bc.
  6. Solve: λ1,2=τ±τ2−4Δ2.
  7. General solution. The equation is linear, so adding two solutions gives another solution: 𝐱(t)=c1eλ1t𝐯1+c2eλ2t𝐯2, where the numbers c1,c2 are fixed by the starting point.

Reading the general solution: each part grows if its λ is positive and shrinks if negative. After a long time the part with the larger λ dominates. That single observation gives every picture in §2.

What to remember
  • 𝐱˙=A𝐱. Try eλt𝐯 and get A𝐯=λ𝐯.
  • λ2−τλ+Δ=0, τ=a+d, Δ=ad−bc.
  • 𝐱(t)=c1eλ1t𝐯1+c2eλ2t𝐯2.
  • x˙=ax, y˙=−y: node (a<0, a≠−1), star (a=−1), line of fixed points (a=0), saddle (a>0).
Check yourself
DrillWithout looking, derive the equation λ2−τλ+Δ=0 starting from 𝐱˙=A𝐱.›

Guess eλt𝐯; substitute to get λeλt𝐯=eλtA𝐯; cancel to get A𝐯=λ𝐯; non-zero 𝐯 needs det⁡(A−λI)=0; expand (a−λ)(d−λ)−bc=λ2−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc).

ConceptFor x˙=−3x, y˙=−y, along which axis do paths enter the origin?›

x shrinks like e−3t, faster than y (e−t). The x-part vanishes first, so paths come in along the y-axis. It is a stable node.

2Classifying a fixed point · saddles, nodes, spirals, centers

Deck 11 · slides 25–28 · Deck 12 · slides 2–5

Every 2-D linear system has the origin as a fixed point, and the shape of the motion around it falls into a few types. You can tell which type from just two numbers, the trace τ and the determinant Δ, without finding any eigenvectors.

What the exam can ask
  • Classify the fixed point of a given matrix (saddle, node, spiral, center) and say whether it is stable.
  • Draw or explain the trace–determinant diagram.

The rules, and why

Everything follows from the eigenvalue formula λ=τ±τ2−4Δ2, together with two facts: λ1λ2=Δ and λ1+λ2=τ.

ConditionEigenvaluesTypeWhy
Δ<0real, one positive, one negativesaddle (always unstable)The product λ1λ2=Δ is negative, so they have opposite signs: one direction grows, one shrinks.
0<Δ<τ2/4real, same signnode: stable if τ<0, unstable if τ>0Positive product means same sign; the sum τ tells you which sign.
Δ>τ2/4complex pair τ2±i⋅spiral: stable if τ<0, unstable if τ>0τ2−4Δ<0, so the square root is imaginary: rotation, with real part τ/2 deciding growth or decay (M3).
τ=0, Δ>0±iΔcenterPure rotation with no growth or decay: closed loops around the origin.
τ2=4Δrepeatedstar or degenerate nodeThe borderline between nodes and spirals.
A slide statement to avoid

The slides say a spiral "rotates counterclockwise for a>d and clockwise for a<d". That rule comes from a book that writes the matrix differently, and it is wrong for 𝐱˙=A𝐱 with A=(abcd). The correct test: look at c. If c>0 the rotation is counterclockwise, if c<0 it is clockwise. (At the point (1,0), y˙=c, so a positive c pushes the point upwards, which is counterclockwise.)

Derivation to learn · classifying a fixed point
  1. Write A, compute τ=a+d and Δ=ad−bc.
  2. If Δ<0: saddle. Stop.
  3. Otherwise compute τ2−4Δ. If negative: spiral (or center if τ=0). If positive: node.
  4. Stability from the sign of τ: negative means stable, positive means unstable.
  5. Back it up by writing the eigenvalues λ=τ±τ2−4Δ2.

  • Δ<0: saddle. Δ>0: node if τ2>4Δ, spiral if τ2<4Δ; stable if τ<0. τ=0, Δ>0: center.

Δ=λ1λ2<0 means one eigenvalue is positive. The part of the solution along that eigenvector grows like eλt, so almost every starting point is pushed away.

3Solving an initial value problem

Deck 11 · slides 16–19

An initial value problem gives the equations and a starting point, and asks for the exact formulas x(t) and y(t). The method: find eigenvalues and eigenvectors, write the general solution, then choose c1,c2 so it starts at the given point. This is the worked example on the slides.

Worked derivation · x˙=x+y, y˙=4x−2y, starting at (2,−3)
  1. Matrix form. A=(114−2), so τ=−1, Δ=(1)(−2)−(1)(4)=−6.
  2. Eigenvalues. λ2+λ−6=0⇒(λ−2)(λ+3)=0⇒λ1=2, λ2=−3.
  3. Eigenvectors. λ=2: first row of A−2I gives −v1+v2=0, so 𝐯1=(1,1). λ=−3: first row of A+3I gives 4v1+v2=0, so 𝐯2=(1,−4).
  4. General solution. (xy)=c1(11)e2t+c2(1−4)e−3t.
  5. Use the starting point. At t=0, e0=1: x(0)=c1+c2=2 and y(0)=c1−4c2=−3. Subtract the second from the first: 5c2=5, so c2=1, and then c1=1.
  6. Answer. x(t)=e2t+e−3t,y(t)=e2t−4e−3t.
  7. Check. At t=0: x=2, y=1−4=−3. Correct. And x˙=2e2t−3e−3t, while x+y=2e2t−3e−3t. Correct.

What it means: Δ<0, so the origin is a saddle (picture in §1). The e−3t part dies away and the e2t part takes over, so the point ends up moving out along the line y=x (the direction 𝐯1). The slide's red note asks what happens if you start at (1,−4) instead. That point is exactly on 𝐯2, so c1=0, c2=1, and the solution (e−3t,−4e−3t) slides straight into the origin, the only way to reach a saddle.

What to remember

Eigenvalues → eigenvectors → general solution → two equations for c1,c2 from t=0 → check by substituting back.

Check yourself
CalcSolve x˙=x+y, y˙=4x−2y starting at (0,5).›

c1+c2=0 and c1−4c2=5. Subtract: 5c2=−5, c2=−1, c1=1. So x=e2t−e−3t, y=e2t+4e−3t. Check at 0: x=0, y=5.

4Romeo and Juliet · a linear system as a story

Deck 11 · slides 21–29

The slides use a love story to practise classification. R(t) is Romeo's love for Juliet and J(t) is Juliet's love for Romeo (positive means love, negative means hate). Each person's feelings change according to their own feelings and the other's. Classifying the fixed point R=J=0 (mutual indifference) tells you how the romance goes.

The fickle Juliet model

Romeo echoes Juliet: he warms up when she loves him (R˙=bJ, b>0). Juliet runs away when he loves her and is attracted when he backs off (J˙=−cR, c>0).

Derivation to learn · the center
  1. A=(0b−c0).
  2. τ=0+0=0. Δ=0⋅0−b(−c)=bc>0.
  3. λ2+bc=0⇒λ=±ibc: purely imaginary.
  4. So the origin is a center: closed loops. Love and hate cycle forever. They love each other at the same time for about a quarter of each cycle (when both R and J are positive).

(The slide writes J˙=−CR with a capital C; it is the same constant c. Since −c<0, the loops run clockwise in the (R,J) plane.)

The general model and the slide's scenario

The general version is R˙=aR+bJ, J˙=cR+dJ. a and d say how each person reacts to their own feelings (enthusiasm if positive, caution if negative); b and c say how they react to the other's.

Worked example · slide 29

Romeo is not afraid to love (a=0.1>0) and is an enthusiastic lover (b=1); Juliet is cautious (c=−1, d=0).

  1. A=(0.11−10), τ=0.1, Δ=0−(1)(−1)=1.
  2. τ2−4Δ=0.01−4=−3.99<0: a spiral. τ>0: unstable.
  3. λ=0.05±i3.992≈0.05±0.999i.

An unstable spiral: the cycles of love and hate grow bigger and bigger over time.

5The Jacobian · zooming in on a fixed point

Deck 11 · slides 31–34, 54–57 · Deck 12 · slides 7–8

Real models are not linear: they contain terms like x2 or xy. Such systems usually cannot be solved exactly. But if you zoom in very close to a fixed point, every smooth curve looks like a straight line, so the system looks linear there. The matrix of that linear approximation is called the Jacobian. Its eigenvalues then classify the fixed point exactly as in §2. This is the most used tool in the second half of the course.

What the exam can ask
  • Derive the linearisation δ𝐱˙=Jδ𝐱 using a Taylor expansion.
  • Write the Jacobian of a given system and evaluate it at each fixed point.
  • State the stability rule in terms of the eigenvalues.

The system

x˙1=f1(x1,x2),x˙2=f2(x1,x2).

A fixed point (x1∗,x2∗) is where both rates are zero: f1(x1∗,x2∗)=0 and f2(x1∗,x2∗)=0.

Derivation to learn · linearisation
  1. Push the system a little. Write x1=x1∗+δx1, x2=x2∗+δx2, where δx1,δx2 are small. Since x1∗ is constant, x˙1=δx˙1.
  2. Taylor-expand each rate (the 2-variable version of "value plus slope times change"): δx˙1=f1(x1∗+δx1, x2∗+δx2)=f1(x1∗,x2∗)+∂f1∂x1δx1+∂f1∂x2δx2+… and the same for f2. The partial derivatives are evaluated at the fixed point.
  3. Use the fixed-point condition. f1(x1∗,x2∗)=0, and the "…" terms (products of small numbers) are tiny, so drop them.
  4. Write it as a matrix: (δx˙1δx˙2)=(∂f1∂x1∂f1∂x2∂f2∂x1∂f2∂x2)⏟J (Jacobian)(δx1δx2).
  5. This is a linear system (§1), so the push behaves like c1eλ1t𝐯1+c2eλ2t𝐯2 with λ the eigenvalues of J.
  6. Stability rule. If every eigenvalue has negative real part, the push dies out: stable. If any has positive real part: unstable. Then classify the type (saddle, node, spiral) with τ and Δ of J, as in §2.

The slide's equation (5) has a small typo: it writes x2∗+δx1 as the second argument; it should be x2∗+δx2. The slide also says stability needs "the largest eigenvalue" negative; strictly it is the largest real part.

One limitation: if the eigenvalues are purely imaginary (a center for J), the linear picture cannot decide what the nonlinear system does, because the tiny dropped terms can tip it either way. The predator–prey model in §7 is an example.

What to remember
  • Jij=∂fi/∂xj, evaluated at the fixed point.
  • All eigenvalues with negative real part: stable. Any with positive real part: unstable.
  • Recipe: fixed points → J in general → J at each point → τ,Δ or eigenvalues → type.

6Rabbits vs sheep · a complete worked analysis

Deck 11 · slides 35–47

Rabbits and sheep eat the same grass. Each would grow to a natural limit on its own, but they compete. Rabbits breed faster; sheep win the fights over grass. The question is whether both can survive together. Working through this model is exactly the kind of long derivation an exam asks for, so it is shown here in full.

What the exam can ask
  • Find all four fixed points, the Jacobian, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors at each fixed point, and classify them.
  • Sketch the phase portrait and explain the outcome (competitive exclusion).
x˙=x(3−x−2y),y˙=y(2−x−y)

x = rabbits, y = sheep. Read the rabbit equation: on their own (y=0) rabbits grow logistically, x˙=x(3−x), up to 3. Each meeting with a sheep costs them, the −2xy term. Sheep on their own grow to 2, and meeting rabbits costs them less, −xy. (Logistic growth is explained in §8 of the Mid-sem guide.)

Derivation to learn · the full analysis
  1. Fixed points. x˙=0 when x=0 or x+2y=3. y˙=0 when y=0 or x+y=2. Combine one condition from each:
    • x=0, y=0: (0,0), nobody.
    • x=0, x+y=2: (0,2), sheep only.
    • x+2y=3, y=0: (3,0), rabbits only.
    • x+2y=3, x+y=2: subtract to get y=1, then x=1. (1,1), both.
  2. Jacobian. Multiply out: f1=3x−x2−2xy, f2=2y−xy−y2. Partial derivatives (M4): J=(3−2x−2y−2x−y2−x−2y).

8Stability of dynamics on a network

Deck 12 · slides 9–24

In the Mid-sem guide (§13) every node of a network had a value that changed over time, influenced by its neighbours. Here we ask: if all the nodes sit at a fixed point and something pushes them slightly, does the network return? The answer depends on two things at once, the dynamics at each node and the shape of the network, and for an important special case it comes down to the largest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian.

What the exam can ask
  • Linearise x˙i=f(xi)+∑jAijg(xi,xj) around a fixed point.
  • For a symmetric fixed point with coupling g(xi,xj)=g(xi)−g(xj), show that 𝝐˙=(αI+βL)𝝐 and derive the stability condition.
  • Apply it to population growth with migration and get λn<r/(2a).

The setting

x˙i=f(xi)+∑jAijg(xi,xj)

xi is the value at node i. f is what the node does alone. g(xi,xj) is the effect of neighbour j on node i; Aij (1 for neighbours, 0 otherwise) keeps only real neighbours. ki=∑jAij is the degree of node i. L is the graph Laplacian, Lij=kiδij−Aij, with eigenvalues 0=λ1≤λ2≤⋯≤λn (Mid-sem guide §12).

A fixed point {xi∗} is a value at every node such that nothing changes: f(xi∗)+∑jAijg(xi∗,xj∗)=0 for every i. Where it sits depends on both the dynamics and the network.

Derivation to learn · from the network model to the Laplacian condition
  1. Push slightly. xi=xi∗+ϵi with small ϵi. Then x˙i=ϵ˙i.
  2. Taylor-expand f and g to first order: ϵ˙i=f(xi∗)+ϵif′(xi∗)+∑jAij[g(xi∗,xj∗)+ϵi∂g∂u+ϵj∂g∂v], where u and v stand for the first and second arguments of g.
  3. The zero-order terms cancel because they add up to the fixed-point condition, which is 0.
  4. Name the slopes (they are just numbers once the fixed point is known): αi=f′(xi∗), βij=∂g/∂u, γij=∂g/∂v. Then ϵ˙i=[αi+∑jβijAij]ϵi+∑jγijAijϵj, a linear system 𝝐˙=M𝝐. Its solution is ∑rar(0)eλrt𝐯r over the eigenvalues of M, as for diffusion. Stable if all eigenvalues of M are negative.
  5. Symmetric fixed point. If every node has the same value, xi∗=x∗, then αi=α, βij=β, γij=γ for all nodes, and ∑jAij=ki: ϵ˙i=(α+βki)ϵi+γ∑jAijϵj.
  6. Difference coupling. If g(xi,xj)=g(xi)−g(xj), then ∂g/∂u=g′(x∗)=β and ∂g/∂v=−g′(x∗)=−β. So γ=−β and ϵ˙i=αϵi+β(kiϵi−∑jAijϵj)=αϵi+β∑j(kiδij−Aij)ϵj.
  7. Matrix form. The bracket is the Laplacian: 𝝐˙=(αI+βL)𝝐.
  8. Eigenvalues. αI+βL has the same eigenvectors as L, with eigenvalues α+βλr. Stability needs α+βλr<0 for every r.
  9. Read off the conditions. λ1=0 gives α<0: each node must be stable on its own. With β>0, the hardest case is the largest eigenvalue λn: α+βλn<0, which rearranges to 1λn>−βα.

The point of the result: whether the network is stable depends on the dynamics (α, β) and on the structure, through one number, the largest Laplacian eigenvalue λn.

Worked example · population growth with migration (slides 21–24)

Each node is a patch where a population grows logistically, f(x)=rx(1−x) with r>0, and individuals migrate between neighbouring patches with g(xi,xj)=axi2−axj2, a>0.

  1. Slopes: f′(x)=r−2rx, g′(x)=2ax.
  2. Symmetric fixed points: all patches equal makes the migration term zero, so f(x∗)=0: x∗=0 or x∗=1.
  3. x∗=0: α=f′(0)=r>0, β=g′(0)=0. Every eigenvalue is r>0: always unstable (any small population starts to grow).
  4. x∗=1: α=f′(1)=r−2r=−r, β=g′(1)=2a. Condition: 1λn>−βα=2ar, so λn<r2a.

  • Logistic + migration: x∗=1 stable iff λn<r/(2a).

9Limit cycles and the Hopf bifurcation

Deck 12 · slides 25–33

A heartbeat, a body-temperature rhythm or a firing neuron oscillates by itself, with no outside push, and returns to the same rhythm after a disturbance. Such a self-sustained oscillation is a limit cycle: a closed loop in the phase plane that nearby paths are pulled onto. The standard example (called the Stuart–Landau oscillator) becomes easy once you switch to polar coordinates, and its birth as a parameter changes is the Hopf bifurcation.

What the exam can ask
  • Convert z˙=(μ+iω−|z|2)z to polar form and find the limit cycle. (2024 mid-sem Q2, 7 marks)
  • Write the Cartesian form, the Jacobian at the origin, and its eigenvalues.
  • Explain the bifurcation at μ=0 (supercritical Hopf), and the difference from subcritical.

What a limit cycle is

A limit cycle is an isolated closed path: the paths next to it are not closed themselves but spiral towards it or away from it. That is the difference from a center (§2), where every nearby path is its own closed loop. A limit cycle is stable if paths on both sides spiral onto it, unstable if they spiral away, and half-stable if one side approaches and the other leaves.

The model

z˙=(μ+iω−|z|2)z,z=x+iy

z is the point (x,y) written as one complex number (M3). μ (Greek "mu") is the parameter we will vary. ω (Greek "omega", written w on the slides) sets the speed of rotation. |z|=x2+y2 is the distance from the origin.

Derivation to learn · polar form and the limit cycle
  1. Polar form. Write z=reiθ, so |z|=r.
  2. Left side. By the product rule (M3): z˙=(r˙+irθ˙)eiθ.
  3. Right side. (μ+iω−r2)reiθ.
  4. Cancel eiθ (never zero) and compare: r˙+irθ˙=(μ−r2)r+iωr.
  5. Match real parts and imaginary parts separately (two complex numbers are equal only if both parts are equal): r˙=(μ−r2)r=μr−r3,θ˙=ω.
  6. The angle just increases at constant speed ω: the point always turns around the origin.
  7. The distance obeys a 1-D equation. Fixed points: r∗=0, and r∗=μ when μ>0 (a distance cannot be negative). Slope: f′(r)=μ−3r2. So f′(0)=μ and f′(μ)=μ−3μ=−2μ.
  8. μ<0: only r∗=0, with f′(0)=μ<0: stable. Every path spirals into the origin.
  9. μ>0: r∗=0 is unstable (f′(0)=μ>0) and r∗=μ is stable (f′(μ)=−2μ<0). Every path (except the origin itself) approaches the circle of radius μ and goes round it at speed ω. That circle is a stable limit cycle.

Notice that r˙=μr−r3 is exactly the supercritical pitchfork from the Mid-sem guide (§9). The slides point this out with a double arrow. The difference is that r cannot be negative, so the two prongs ±μ of the pitchfork become one circle.

The same system in x,y, and its Jacobian

Multiplying out (μ+iω−(x2+y2))(x+iy) and separating real and imaginary parts:

x˙=μx−ωy−x(x2+y2),y˙=ωx+μy−y(x2+y2).
Derivation to learn · the origin is an unstable spiral (slides 30–31, μ=1)
  1. With μ=1: f1=x−ωy−x3−xy2, f2=ωx+y−x2y−y3.
  2. Jacobian: J=(1−3x2−y2−ω−2xyω−2xy1−x2−3y2).
  3. At (0,0): J=(1−ωω1), τ=2, Δ=1+ω2.
  4. λ=2±4−4(1+ω2)2=1±iω. Positive real part with rotation: unstable spiral.
  5. So paths starting near the origin spiral away from it, consistent with r˙=r−r3>0 for small r. They end on the circle r=1.

The Hopf bifurcation

As μ passes 0, the eigenvalues at the origin, μ±iω, cross from the left half (negative real part) to the right half of the complex plane, and a limit cycle is born. A stable point turning into a stable oscillation this way is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation. The cycle's radius grows like μ.

The slides also give the general form z˙=(a+iω+b|z|2)z with b=α+iβ. In polar form r˙=(a+αr2)r.

  • α<0: supercritical. For a>0 a stable cycle of radius −a/α exists around an unstable origin. (Our model is α=−1.)
  • α>0: subcritical. For a<0 an unstable cycle surrounds a stable origin; as a passes 0 the cycle shrinks onto the origin and disappears, and for a>0 paths spiral out with nothing nearby to stop them. This is the 2-D version of the subcritical pitchfork: a sudden jump.
What to remember
  • z=reiθ: z˙=(μ+iω−|z|2)z becomes r˙=μr−r3, θ˙=ω.
  • μ>0: stable limit cycle r=μ, unstable origin. μ<0: stable origin.
  • Jacobian at the origin: eigenvalues μ±iω. Supercritical Hopf at μ=0.

10Van der Pol, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, and networks of oscillators

Deck 12 · slides 34–43

Most limit cycles are not perfect circles and cannot be found with a formula; they are found by simulation. This section covers the classic example (van der Pol), a theorem that proves a limit cycle exists without finding it (Poincaré–Bendixson), and what happens when many oscillators are coupled on a network and some of them stop working.

The van der Pol oscillator

x¨+μ(x2−1)x˙+x=0

x¨ is the acceleration (slope of the slope). Without the middle term this is a simple spring, x¨=−x. The middle term is a "friction" that changes sign: for small |x|<1 it is negative (it pumps energy in, so small swings grow), and for large |x|>1 it is positive (it removes energy, so large swings shrink). The balance is a limit cycle. Written as two first-order equations with v=x˙: x˙=v, v˙=−x−μ(x2−1)v.

The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

Statement

If a path stays forever inside a closed, bounded region of the plane that contains no fixed points, then the path must approach a closed orbit.

How to use it: build a trapping region, a ring-shaped area such that on both its inner and outer edges the flow points into the ring. Then every path inside stays inside. If there is no fixed point in the ring, the theorem guarantees a closed orbit in it.

Worked example · the model of §9 with μ=1

Take the ring 0.5≤r≤2. On the inner edge r=0.5: r˙=0.5−0.125=0.375>0, the flow points outwards, into the ring. On the outer edge r=2: r˙=2−8=−6<0, the flow points inwards, into the ring. The only fixed point is the origin, which is outside the ring. So by Poincaré–Bendixson there is a closed orbit in the ring. (We know it is r=1.)

The theorem works only in two dimensions. In three dimensions paths can wander forever without settling, which is how chaos becomes possible (§14).

Networks of oscillators that "age" (slides 38–43)

The slides couple N Stuart–Landau oscillators all-to-all:

z˙i=(μi+iω−|zi|2)zi+KN∑k=1N(zk−zi).

A fraction p of the oscillators are "inactive" (μ<0: alone they would stop at 0), the rest "active" (μ>0: alone they oscillate). The collective oscillation is measured by the order parameter Z=1N∑jzj, and Q=|Z(p)|/|Z(0)| compares it with the case of no inactive oscillators. The simulation on the slides (N=50) shows Q falling as p grows; with strong coupling (K=30) it drops to exactly 0 at about p≈0.7: the whole network stops oscillating. This is the aging transition (Daido and Nakanishi, 2004). Related papers on the slides show that in networks with hubs, losing low-degree nodes can kill the collective oscillation (Tanaka et al., 2012), and that targeted interventions control epidemics in patch networks better than random ones (Tanaka et al., 2014).

The plot on slide 41 labels μa=−0.5, μia=1; by slide 40's definitions these look swapped (inactive should be negative).

What to remember
  • Van der Pol: x¨+μ(x2−1)x˙+x=0; negative damping for small x, positive for large x, limit cycle in between.
  • Poincaré–Bendixson: trapping region with no fixed point ⇒ closed orbit. Only in 2-D.
  • Aging transition: enough inactive oscillators stop the whole network.

11From a differential equation to a map

Deck 13 · slides 1–2, 10–13

Computers solve differential equations by taking small steps in time. If the step is too large, the numerical answer can behave completely differently from the true one. The slides use this to connect the smooth logistic equation to the logistic map, a step-by-step rule that can behave chaotically. This section also gives the Runge–Kutta method that the slides list.

Euler's step and the logistic map

Euler's method replaces the slope by "change over one step": x˙≈xn+1−xnΔt, so xn+1=xn+Δt⋅f(xn).

Derivation to learn · logistic equation → logistic map (slide 11)
  1. Start from x˙=x(K−1)−Kx2, a logistic equation with fixed point x∗=K−1K (set x[(K−1)−Kx]=0).
  2. Euler step: xn+1=xn+Δt[(K−1)xn−Kxn2].
  3. Take the step size Δt=1: xn+1=xn+(K−1)xn−Kxn2=Kxn−Kxn2.
  4. So xn+1=Kxn(1−xn): the logistic map, with r=K. Its non-zero fixed point 1−1/K is the same as the equation's.

The smooth equation always settles at its fixed point. The map, as §12 and §13 show, can oscillate or become chaotic when K is large. The difference comes only from the large step. That is the point of the slide's question "what will be the ideal step size?": too large a step creates behaviour that is not in the real system.

For reference, the logistic equation N˙=rN(1−N/K) has the exact solution N(t)=K1+Ae−rt with A=K−N0N0, the same S-shaped curve as the SIS solution in the Mid-sem guide.

The fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (RK4)

Euler uses the slope at the start of each step only. RK4 measures the slope at four places in the step and takes a weighted average:

k1=f(tj, yj)(slope at the start)k2=f(tj+h2, yj+h2k1)(slope at the middle, using k1)k3=f(tj+h2, yj+h2k2)(slope at the middle, using k2)k4=f(tj+h, yj+hk3)(slope at the end)yj+1=yj+h(k16+k23+k33+k46)

The weights add to 1. The error for a fixed time span shrinks like the step size Δt for Euler, (Δt)2 for improved Euler, and (Δt)4 for RK4. Halving the step makes RK4 about 16 times more accurate.

What to remember
  • Euler with Δt=1 turns x˙=x(K−1)−Kx2 into xn+1=Kxn(1−xn).
  • RK4: four slopes, weights 16,13,13,16. Error orders: Euler Δt, improved Euler (Δt)2, RK4 (Δt)4.

12Stability of a map, and cobweb diagrams

Deck 13 · slides 13–21

For a map xn+1=f(xn), a fixed point is a value that maps to itself. Whether nearby values are pulled in or pushed away depends on the slope f′(x∗), but the rule is different from differential equations: what matters is whether the slope's size is less than 1. A cobweb diagram lets you see the iteration on paper.

What the exam can ask
  • Derive the condition for a stable fixed point of xn+1=f(xn). (2024 Quiz 2, 2 marks, both sets)
  • Find the fixed points and their stability for f(x)=x2, f(x)=cos⁡x, f(x)=rx(1−x). (2024 Quiz 2, 3 marks)
Derivation to learn · stability of a map's fixed point
  1. Fixed point: x∗=f(x∗).
  2. Small error. Write xn=x∗+εn, with εn small.
  3. One step. x∗+εn+1=f(x∗+εn)≈f(x∗)+f′(x∗)εn (value plus slope times change).
  4. Cancel x∗=f(x∗) from both sides: εn+1=f′(x∗)εn.
  5. Repeat n times: εn=[f′(x∗)]nε0. Each step multiplies the error by the same number.
  6. Conclusion. Repeated multiplication by a number shrinks to 0 only if the number's size is below 1: |f′(x∗)|<1 ⇒ stable,|f′(x∗)|>1 ⇒ unstable,|f′(x∗)|=1 ⇒ bifurcation point.

Compare with a differential equation (Mid-sem §8), where only the sign of the slope mattered. A map with slope −0.5 is stable (error halves and flips sign each step), but a map with slope −2 is unstable even though the slope is negative. A negative slope makes the iterates jump from one side of x∗ to the other.

Worked examples

f(x)=x2. Fixed points: x=x2⇒x(x−1)=0⇒x∗=0,1. Slope f′(x)=2x. |f′(0)|=0<1: stable. |f′(1)|=2>1: unstable. (Start at 0.9: 0.81, 0.66, 0.43, … goes to 0. Start at 1.1: 1.21, 1.46, … runs away.)

f(x)=cos⁡x (x in radians). Fixed point: x∗=cos⁡x∗, solved by repeated substitution: start at 1: cos⁡1=0.540, cos⁡0.540=0.858, 0.654, 0.794, … settling at x∗≈0.739. Slope f′(x)=−sin⁡x, |f′(0.739)|=sin⁡0.739≈0.674<1: stable. Negative slope, so the iterates zig-zag around 0.739 as they converge.

f(x)=rx(1−x), the logistic map. Fixed points: x=rx(1−x)⇒x∗=0 or 1=r(1−x)⇒x∗=1−1r. Slope f′(x)=r(1−2x).

  • f′(0)=r: stable if r<1 (the population dies out).
  • f′(1−1r)=r(1−2+2r)=2−r: stable if |2−r|<1, that is 1<r<3.
  • At r=3, f′=−1: a bifurcation (§13).

Numbers: r=2.5 gives x∗=1−0.4=0.6 with slope 2−2.5=−0.5, stable. The slide's table confirms it: starting from 0.5 the iterates go 0.625, 0.586, 0.607, 0.597, … → 0.6.

Cobweb diagrams

Draw the curve y=f(x) and the diagonal y=x. Start at x0 on the horizontal axis. Go vertically to the curve (that height is x1=f(x0)), then horizontally to the diagonal (which moves you to x1 on the horizontal axis). Repeat. Fixed points are where the curve meets the diagonal.

What to remember
  • Map fixed point x∗=f(x∗), stable iff |f′(x∗)|<1.
  • Logistic map: x∗=0 stable for r<1; x∗=1−1/r with f′=2−r, stable for 1<r<3.
  • cos⁡x: x∗=0.739, stable. x2: 0 stable, 1 unstable.

13Period doubling and the route to chaos

Deck 13 · slides 14, 22–26

As r increases past 3, the logistic map stops settling on one value and instead alternates between two values (a 2-cycle). Further on it alternates between 4, then 8, and so on, faster and faster, until about r=3.57 where the values never repeat: chaos. This sequence is the period-doubling route to chaos.

Derivation to learn · the 2-cycle
  1. A 2-cycle is a pair x1,x2 with f(x1)=x2 and f(x2)=x1. So each is a fixed point of the twice-applied map: f(f(x))=x.
  2. For the logistic map this is a degree-4 equation. Two of its solutions are the ordinary fixed points (0 and 1−1/r), which also satisfy it. Dividing those out leaves a quadratic, whose solutions are x±=(r+1)±(r−3)(r+1)2r.
  3. These are real only if (r−3)(r+1)≥0, that is r≥3. So the 2-cycle is born at r=3, exactly where the fixed point loses stability (its slope reaches −1). This is a period-doubling bifurcation.
  4. Numbers, r=3.2: 0.2×4.2=0.84=0.9165. x±=4.2±0.91656.4=0.7995 and 0.5130. The slide's table shows the iterates from 0.5 going 0.800, 0.512, 0.7995, 0.5129, … exactly these two values.

15Two coupled oscillators

Deck 13 · slides 28–31 · 2024 mid-sem Q1

In 1665 Huygens noticed that two pendulum clocks hanging from the same beam end up swinging in step. Heart pacemaker cells, fireflies flashing together and audiences clapping in rhythm do the same: oscillators that interact weakly tend to synchronise. This section shows how two coupled limit-cycle oscillators reduce to equations for their angles (phases) alone, then finds when they lock to a common frequency.

What the exam can ask
  • Write two coupled Stuart–Landau oscillators in polar form and explain the weak-coupling (phase-only) approximation.
  • For two phase oscillators, find the critical coupling for a common frequency, and the common frequency. (2024 mid-sem Q1, 7 marks)

From oscillators to phases

Two limit-cycle oscillators (§9 with μ=1), each pulled towards the other with strength K:

Z˙1=(1+iω1−|Z1|2)Z1+K(Z2−Z1),Z˙2=(1+iω2−|Z2|2)Z2+K(Z1−Z2).
Derivation to learn · polar form of the coupled pair
  1. Write Zj=rjeiθj. Then Z˙1=(r˙1+ir1θ˙1)eiθ1.
  2. Divide the first equation by eiθ1: r˙1+ir1θ˙1=(1+iω1−r12)r1−Kr1+Kr2ei(θ2−θ1). (Because Z2/eiθ1=r2ei(θ2−θ1).)
  3. Use eiϕ=cos⁡ϕ+isin⁡ϕ and match real parts: r˙1=r1(1−K−r12)+Kr2cos⁡(θ2−θ1).
  4. Match imaginary parts and divide by r1: θ˙1=ω1+Kr2r1sin⁡(θ2−θ1). The same for oscillator 2 with 1 and 2 swapped.
  5. Weak coupling. The sizes r1,r2 relax quickly back to the limit cycle, while the phases interact slowly. So set r1≈r2≈ constant. Then r2/r1=1 and only the phases remain: θ˙1=ω1+Ksin⁡(θ2−θ1),θ˙2=ω2+Ksin⁡(θ1−θ2).

This is the two-oscillator Kuramoto model. Each phase moves at its own natural speed ω, plus a pull towards the other: if oscillator 2 is ahead (θ2>θ1 by a little), sin⁡(θ2−θ1)>0 speeds oscillator 1 up.

When do they lock?

The 2024 mid-sem used different couplings: θ˙1=ω1+k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1), θ˙2=ω2+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2).

Derivation to learn · locking condition and common frequency
  1. Phase difference. Let ϕ=θ2−θ1. Subtract the equations, using sin⁡(θ1−θ2)=−sin⁡ϕ: ϕ˙=(ω2−ω1)−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ.
  2. Locking means a common frequency, so θ1 and θ2 grow at the same rate and ϕ stops changing: a fixed point of the ϕ equation. ϕ˙=0 gives sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−ω1k1+k2.
  3. A sine is between −1 and 1, so a solution exists only if |ω2−ω1|≤k1+k2. The critical coupling is k1+k2=|ω2−ω1|(for k1=k2=k: kc=|ω2−ω1|/2).
  4. Which fixed point is stable. The slope of ϕ˙ with respect to ϕ is −(k1+k2)cos⁡ϕ. Negative (stable) when cos⁡ϕ∗>0, the solution with ϕ∗ between −90° and 90°.
  5. The common frequency Ω. When locked, θ˙1=θ˙2=Ω: Ω=ω1+k1sin⁡ϕ∗ and Ω=ω2−k2sin⁡ϕ∗. Multiply the first by k2, the second by k1, and add, so the sin⁡ϕ∗ terms cancel: (k1+k2)Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2⇒Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2. With equal couplings this is the plain average (ω1+ω2)/2.
With the exam's numbers · k1=k2=2, ω1=2, ω2=10
  1. Difference of natural frequencies: |ω2−ω1|=8.
  2. Total coupling: k1+k2=4.
  3. Locking needs 8≤4, which is false. sin⁡ϕ∗=8/4=2 has no solution.
  4. So there is no common, stable frequency: the phase difference keeps increasing and the oscillators drift. They would need k1+k2≥8 (each k≥4). If they did lock, the frequency would be Ω=(2×2+2×10)/4=6.

The loss of locking at k1+k2=|Δω| is a saddle-node bifurcation of the ϕ equation: the stable and unstable values of ϕ∗ meet at 90° and disappear.

What to remember
  • ϕ˙=Δω−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ.
  • Lock iff |ω2−ω1|≤k1+k2. Common frequency Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2.

16The Kuramoto model · many oscillators

Deck 13 · slides 33–37, 46–48

Now take N oscillators, each with its own natural frequency, every one pulled towards every other. With weak coupling they ignore each other and drift; above a critical coupling a group of them starts moving together. Kuramoto's trick is to measure "how together" they are with one number, the order parameter, and to rewrite each oscillator's equation in terms of it.

What the exam can ask
  • Define the order parameter reiψ and explain r and ψ.
  • Derive the mean-field form θ˙i=ωi+Krsin⁡(ψ−θi).
  • State the critical coupling Kc=2/(πg(0)).
θ˙i=ωi+KN∑j=1Nsin⁡(θj−θi),i=1,…,N

θi is the phase (angle) of oscillator i; ωi its natural frequency, drawn from a distribution g(ω) that is symmetric about 0, g(ω)=g(−ω); K the coupling strength.

The order parameter

reiψ=1N∑j=1Neiθj

Think of each oscillator as a dot on a circle of radius 1 at angle θj. The right side is the average position of the dots. Its distance from the centre, r, measures coherence: r=1 if all dots are at the same angle (perfect sync), r≈0 if they are spread evenly (no sync). Its angle ψ is the average phase.

Derivation to learn · the mean-field equation
  1. Multiply the definition by e−iθi: rei(ψ−θi)=1N∑jei(θj−θi).
  2. Take the imaginary part of both sides (eia has imaginary part sin⁡a): rsin⁡(ψ−θi)=1N∑jsin⁡(θj−θi).
  3. The right side is exactly the coupling sum in the model (divided by K). Substitute: θ˙i=ωi+Krsin⁡(ψ−θi).
  4. Meaning: each oscillator now feels only the average (the "mean field"), pulled towards the average phase ψ with strength Kr. The more synchronised the population (larger r), the stronger the pull, which synchronises it further.
  5. Rotating frame. Measure all phases relative to the average, so ψ=0: θ˙i=ωi−Krsin⁡θi.
  6. Who locks. Oscillator i stops drifting when θ˙i=0, i.e. sin⁡θi=ωi/(Kr), possible only if |ωi|≤Kr. Oscillators with natural frequency close to the average lock; the others keep drifting (partial synchronisation).

The critical coupling

Kc=2πg(0)

Below Kc, r=0 (incoherence). Above it, r grows continuously from 0, a phase transition like the giant component in random graphs. g(0) is the height of the frequency distribution at its centre: the more oscillators have similar frequencies (tall, narrow g), the smaller the coupling needed.

For the Lorentzian distribution on the slides, g(ω)=γ/πγ2+ω2, we get g(0)=1πγ, so Kc=2γ, and the exact result above it is r=1−Kc/K. Example: γ=1 gives Kc=2; at K=4, r=0.5=0.71.

Slide error and exam scope

The derivation of Kc for infinitely many oscillators (slides 39 to 46) is marked "Advanced Topics (Not for exam)" on slide 38, so learn the result, not the derivation.

Explosive synchronisation (slides 47–48)

On a network, the coupling uses the adjacency matrix: θ˙i=ωi+λ∑jAijsin⁡(θj−θi). In the paper on the slides (Gómez-Gardeñes et al., 2011) each node's natural frequency equals its degree. On random (ER) networks r rises smoothly with λ. On scale-free (Barabási–Albert) networks it jumps suddenly from about 0 to about 0.9, at a different λ when increasing than when decreasing: a first-order, "explosive" transition with hysteresis.

What to remember
  • reiψ=1N∑jeiθj: r = coherence (0 to 1), ψ = average phase.
  • θ˙i=ωi+Krsin⁡(ψ−θi); rotating frame θ˙i=ωi−Krsin⁡θi; locked if |ωi|≤Kr.
  • Kc=2/(πg(0)); Lorentzian: Kc=2γ, r=1−Kc/K.

17Will a large system be stable?

Deck 13 · slides 50–71

An ecosystem has hundreds of species, each affecting many others. Writing down every interaction is impossible. In 1972 Robert May asked a different question: if the interactions were random, how likely is the whole system to be stable? His answer, using a result about the eigenvalues of large random matrices, is that too many species, too many connections or too-strong interactions make stability impossible.

What the exam can ask
  • Define the community matrix and explain the stability condition on its eigenvalues.
  • Derive the Jacobian of the generalised Lotka–Volterra model, M=D(x∗)A.
  • State and explain May's criterion SCσ2<d, and the effect of predator–prey vs competition/mutualism pairs.

The community matrix

For many species, x˙i=fi(𝐱). An equilibrium 𝐱∗ has fi(𝐱∗)=0 for every species. Exactly as in §5, a small push Δ𝐱 obeys dΔ𝐱dt≈MΔ𝐱, where M=J|𝐱∗ is the Jacobian at the equilibrium, called the community matrix. Its entry Mij says how a small increase of species j changes the growth of species i. The solution is Δ𝐱(t)=eMtΔ𝐱(0), a combination of eλt terms over the eigenvalues of M. Stability depends on the eigenvalue furthest to the right: stable if its real part is negative.

Slide 52 writes the expansion as f(Δx(0)); it should be f(𝐱∗+Δ𝐱(0)). Slide 54 writes Δx(0)eMt; the matrix should come first, eMtΔ𝐱(0).

Derivation to learn · the GLV community matrix
  1. Generalised Lotka–Volterra model: x˙i=xi(ri+∑jaijxj), or in matrix form 𝐱˙=D(𝐱)(𝐫+A𝐱), where D(𝐱) is the diagonal matrix with 𝐱 on the diagonal, ri the growth rate of species i alone, and aij the effect of species j on species i.
  2. Off-diagonal entries (j≠i): only the term xiaijxj contains xj, so ∂fi∂xj=aijxi.
  3. Diagonal entries: by the product rule, ∂fi∂xi=(ri+∑jaijxj)+aiixi.
  4. At an equilibrium where every species is present, the bracket is zero (that is what x˙i=0 with xi≠0 means). So every entry is Mij=aijxi∗: M=D(𝐱∗)A.

May's random matrix and his criterion

May skipped the details and built the community matrix at random, for S species:

  • Each off-diagonal entry is 0 with probability 1−C (species do not interact), and with probability C it is a random number with mean 0 and variance σ2. C is the connectance, the fraction of possible interactions that exist.
  • Each diagonal entry is −d (self-regulation, like a carrying capacity).
Derivation to learn · May's criterion
  1. Circular law (a theorem about large random matrices): if an S×S matrix has independent entries with mean 0 and variance 1, the eigenvalues of the matrix divided by S fill a disc of radius 1 in the complex plane, evenly.
  2. May's off-diagonal entries have mean 0 and variance Cσ2 (zero with probability 1−C, variance σ2 otherwise). Dividing them by Cσ2 gives variance 1.
  3. So by the circular law, the off-diagonal part has eigenvalues in a disc of radius SCσ2=SCσ2, centred at 0.
  4. Adding −d on the diagonal shifts every eigenvalue left by d: the disc is now centred at −d.
  5. The rightmost eigenvalue is at about −d+SCσ2. Stability needs this to be negative: SCσ2<d.

Meaning: more species S, more connections C or stronger interactions σ all make the disc bigger, and past a point it crosses into the unstable right half. Complexity destabilises, unless self-regulation d is strong.

Numbers

S=100 species, C=0.1, σ=0.5, d=1. SCσ2=100×0.1×0.25=2.5=1.58>1: unstable. With C=0.03: 0.75=0.87<1: stable.

Slide 61 writes the circular-law conditions as 𝔼[Xij]=0 and 𝔼[Xij]=1; the second should be 𝔼[Xij2]=1 (unit variance).

The elliptic law · interaction types matter (Allesina and Tang, 2012)

If the pair (Mij,Mji) is correlated with correlation ρ, the disc becomes an ellipse with horizontal half-width SCσ2(1+ρ), and the criterion becomes SCσ2(1+ρ)<d.

  • Predator–prey pairs (one positive, one negative, so ρ<0): the ellipse is tall and narrow, stretched along the imaginary axis. The criterion is easier to meet: predator–prey interactions stabilise.
  • Competition or mutualism pairs (same signs, ρ>0): the ellipse is wide and flat, reaching further right. Harder to meet: these interactions destabilise.

The slide formulas SC<θ/(1∓𝔼2(|X|)/σ2) are the same criterion with θ=d/σ and ρ=∓𝔼2(|X|)/σ2.

Real networks are not random (Meena et al., 2023)

The last paper on the slides argues that Jacobians of real network dynamics are not random: their entries scale with node degrees, Jii∼−Cdiμ and Jij∼diνAijdjρ, with exponents set by the dynamics (epidemic, gene regulation, population, and so on). A single number built from these exponents and the network's degree distribution sorts systems into three classes: always unstable, sensitive (stable only for some parameters), and stable, where large, heterogeneous networks are guaranteed stable. So scale and hubs can help stability, the opposite of May's random-matrix picture.

What to remember
  • Community matrix M=J|𝐱∗; stable iff rightmost eigenvalue has negative real part.
  • GLV: 𝐱˙=D(𝐱)(𝐫+A𝐱), M=D(𝐱∗)A.
  • May: stable iff SCσ2<d. Elliptic law: SCσ2(1+ρ)<d; predator–prey (ρ<0) stabilises.

18The double pendulum

Deck 13 · slides 73–79

A double pendulum is one pendulum hanging from the end of another. For large swings it is a classic chaotic system. For small swings it behaves simply: its motion is a mix of two "normal modes", one where the two parts swing together and one where they swing opposite each other. The slides derive these from the energy (the Lagrangian method).

Setting up

Two rods of length L1,L2 with masses m1,m2 at their ends. ϕ1 and ϕ2 are the angles of the rods from the vertical. g is the acceleration due to gravity.

  • Positions: x1=L1sin⁡ϕ1, y1=−L1cos⁡ϕ1, x2=x1+L2sin⁡ϕ2, y2=y1−L2cos⁡ϕ2.
  • Kinetic energy T=12(m1+m2)L12ϕ˙12+m22L22ϕ˙22+m2L1L2ϕ˙1ϕ˙2cos⁡(ϕ1−ϕ2).
  • Potential energy V=−(m1+m2)gL1cos⁡ϕ1−m2gL2cos⁡ϕ2.
  • The Lagrangian is ℒ=T−V, and the equations of motion come from ddt∂ℒ∂ϕ˙i−∂ℒ∂ϕi=0.
Derivation to learn · small swings and normal modes
  1. Small angles. Use cos⁡(ϕ1−ϕ2)≈1 in T and cos⁡ϕ≈1−ϕ22 in V. The equations become linear: (m1+m2)L12ϕ¨1+m2L1L2ϕ¨2+(m1+m2)gL1ϕ1=0, m2L1L2ϕ¨1+m2L22ϕ¨2+m2gL2ϕ2=0.
  2. Matrix form. M𝝓¨=−K𝝓 with M=((m1+m2)L12m2L1L2m2L1L2m2L22), K=((m1+m2)gL100m2gL2).
  3. Try an oscillation 𝝓=𝐇eiωt: then 𝝓¨=−ω2𝝓, so (K−ω2M)𝐇=0, which needs det⁡(K−ω2M)=0.

  5. Equal masses (μ=1): ω1,2=gL2±2.
  6. Mode shapes. The fast mode ω1 has 𝐇=(1, −(1+μ)/μ): the two rods swing in opposite directions. The slow mode ω2 has (1, +(1+μ)/μ): they swing together. The general small motion is A1𝐇1cos⁡(ω1t+α1)+A2𝐇2cos⁡(ω2t+α2).

Quiz 2 · Set 1 · Q3 · 2 + 3 marksIf xn+1=f(xn), find the condition (show the derivation) where the system shows a stable fixed point xn+1=f(xn)=x∗. For (i) f(x)=x2 and (ii) f(x)=cos⁡x, find the fixed points and the stability conditions.›

The idea in plain words. A fixed point of a map is a value that maps to itself. Start a tiny distance away and apply the map once: the distance gets multiplied by the slope at the fixed point. Repeating multiplies again and again, so the distance dies out only if that slope is smaller than 1 in size.

Formulas you need

  • Fixed point x∗=f(x∗). Taylor: f(x∗+ε)≈f(x∗)+f′(x∗)ε.
  • Stable iff |f′(x∗)|<1 (§12).

Step by step

  1. Derivation. xn=x∗+εn. Then x∗+εn+1=f(x∗+εn)≈x∗+f′(x∗)εn, so εn+1=f′(x∗)εn and εn=[f′(x∗)]nε0. This goes to 0 iff |f′(x∗)|<1.
  2. (i) f(x)=x2. x=x2⇒x∗=0 or 1. f′(x)=2x. |f′(0)|=0<1: stable. |f′(1)|=2>1: unstable. Numbers: from 0.9 the iterates are 0.81, 0.66, 0.43, 0.19 → 0; from 1.1 they are 1.21, 1.46, 2.14 → ∞.
  3. (ii) f(x)=cos⁡x. x∗=cos⁡x∗. Solve by iterating from 1: 0.540, 0.858, 0.654, 0.794, 0.701, 0.764, … → x∗≈0.739. f′(x)=−sin⁡x, |f′(0.739)|=sin⁡(0.739)=0.674<1: stable. The slope is negative, so the iterates alternate above and below 0.739 while converging.

Final answer

Stable iff |f′(x∗)|<1. (i) x∗=0 stable, x∗=1 unstable. (ii) x∗≈0.739, stable since |−sin⁡0.739|≈0.67<1.

Quiz 2 · Set 2 · Q3 · 2 + 3 marksIf xn+1=f(xn), find the condition where the system shows a stable fixed point. If f(xn)=rxn(1−xn), find the fixed points and the stability conditions.›

The idea in plain words. Same rule as the previous question: stable when the slope at the fixed point is smaller than 1 in size. For the logistic map there are two fixed points, "extinct" and "settled population", and the slope at each depends on r. That gives the range of r where each one is stable.

Formulas you need

  • Stable iff |f′(x∗)|<1.
  • f(x)=rx−rx2, so f′(x)=r−2rx=r(1−2x).

Step by step

  1. Condition: derive εn+1=f′(x∗)εn as in the previous answer.
  2. Fixed points. x=rx(1−x). Either x∗=0, or divide by x: 1=r(1−x)⇒x∗=1−1r.
  3. At x∗=0: f′(0)=r. Stable iff r<1 (for r>0).
  4. At x∗=1−1/r: f′=r(1−2+2r)=2−r. Stable iff |2−r|<1⟺1<r<3.
  5. Numbers. r=0.5: only 0 is stable (0.5 → 0.125 → 0.055 → … → 0). r=2.5: x∗=0.6, slope −0.5, stable (0.5 → 0.625 → 0.586 → 0.607 → 0.6). r=3.2: slope −1.2, unstable; the map settles on the 2-cycle 0.7995, 0.5130.
  6. At r=3 the slope is −1: a period-doubling bifurcation (§13).

Final answer

Fixed points x∗=0 (stable for 0<r<1) and x∗=1−1/r (stable for 1<r<3). At r=3 it loses stability to a 2-cycle.

Quiz 2 · Set 2 · Q4 · 4 marksFind the fixed points of the Lorenz oscillator x˙=σ(y−x), y˙=ρx−y−xz, z˙=−βz+xy. Construct the associated Jacobians. (Eigenvalue analysis is not required.)›

The idea in plain words. Set all three rates to zero and solve one equation at a time: the first says y=x, the third then gives z in terms of x, and the middle one either forces x=0 or fixes z=ρ−1. Then the Jacobian is the table of partial derivatives, evaluated at each fixed point.

Formulas you need

  • Fixed point: all rates zero. Jacobian Jij=∂fi/∂xj (§5, §14).

Step by step

  1. σ(y−x)=0⇒y=x.
  2. xy−βz=0⇒z=x2/β.
  3. ρx−x−xz=x(ρ−1−z)=0⇒x=0 or z=ρ−1.
  4. x=0: P0=(0,0,0). z=ρ−1: x2=β(ρ−1), P1,2=(±β(ρ−1),±β(ρ−1),ρ−1), existing for ρ>1.
  5. General Jacobian: J=(−σσ0ρ−z−1−xyx−β).
  6. J(P0)=(−σσ0ρ−1000−β). J(P1,2)=(−σσ01−1∓c±c±c−β) with c=β(ρ−1).
  7. Numbers, σ=10, β=8/3, ρ=28: c=72=8.49, P1,2=(±8.49,±8.49,27), J(P1)=(−101001−1−8.498.498.49−2.67).

Final answer

P0=(0,0,0) and, for ρ>1, P1,2=(±β(ρ−1),±β(ρ−1),ρ−1), with the Jacobians above.

Mid-sem · Q1 · 3 + 4 marksTwo coupled phase oscillators: θ˙1=ω1+k1sin⁡(θ2−θ1), θ˙2=ω2+k2sin⁡(θ1−θ2). (a) Calculate the critical coupling strength at which both oscillators follow the common frequency. (b) Calculate the common frequency. Can one obtain a common (and stable) frequency at k1=k2=k=2, ω1=2, ω2=10?›

The idea in plain words. The oscillators share a frequency when the gap between their angles stops changing. The gap changes because of the difference in natural speeds, and the coupling pulls against it, but the pull is at most k1+k2 (a sine is never more than 1). So they can lock only if the coupling is at least as big as the speed difference.

Formulas you need

  • ϕ=θ2−θ1, ϕ˙=(ω2−ω1)−(k1+k2)sin⁡ϕ (§15).
  • |sin⁡ϕ|≤1.

Step by step

  1. Subtract the equations to get ϕ˙ above.
  2. Locked means ϕ˙=0: sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−ω1k1+k2. Needs |ω2−ω1|≤k1+k2. Critical coupling: k1+k2=|ω2−ω1|.
  3. Stable lock: slope −(k1+k2)cos⁡ϕ∗<0, i.e. cos⁡ϕ∗>0.
  4. Common frequency: from Ω=ω1+k1sin⁡ϕ∗=ω2−k2sin⁡ϕ∗, eliminate sin⁡ϕ∗: Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2.
  5. Numbers. |ω2−ω1|=8, k1+k2=4. sin⁡ϕ∗=2 is impossible. No lock.

Final answer

(a) Lock iff k1+k2≥|ω2−ω1|; critical coupling k1+k2=|ω2−ω1| (equal couplings: kc=|ω2−ω1|/2). (b) Ω=k2ω1+k1ω2k1+k2. With k=2, ω1=2, ω2=10: 4<8, so no common stable frequency; each k would need to be at least 4. (If they locked, Ω would be 6.)

Mid-sem · Q2 · 3 + 4 marksz˙=(μ+iω−|z|2)z, with z=x+iy. (a) Transform this equation into the (r,θ) plane. (b) Find the condition in which the system will reveal a stable limit cycle. Explain it with proper bifurcation analysis.›

The idea in plain words. Describe the point by its distance from the centre and its angle. The angle just keeps turning. The distance follows the pitchfork equation: it shrinks to 0 when μ is negative, and settles at μ when μ is positive. Turning forever at a fixed distance is a circle, the limit cycle.

Formulas you need

  • z=reiθ, z˙=(r˙+irθ˙)eiθ (M3).
  • 1-D stability: sign of the slope (Mid-sem §8).

Step by step

  1. Substitute: (r˙+irθ˙)eiθ=(μ+iω−r2)reiθ. Cancel eiθ.
  2. Real parts: r˙=μr−r3. Imaginary parts: θ˙=ω.
  3. Radial fixed points r∗=0, and r∗=μ for μ>0. Slope μ−3r2: at 0 it is μ; at μ it is −2μ.
  4. Numbers. μ=−1: r∗=0 with slope −1, stable; paths spiral in. μ=4, ω=1: origin unstable (slope 4), circle r=2 stable (slope −8), one turn every 2π≈6.3 time units.
  5. The eigenvalues at the origin are μ±iω (Cartesian Jacobian, §9); they cross into the right half-plane at μ=0.

Final answer

(a) r˙=μr−r3, θ˙=ω. (b) A stable limit cycle of radius μ exists when μ>0. At μ=0 the stable origin becomes unstable and the cycle is born: a supercritical Hopf bifurcation (the radial equation is a supercritical pitchfork).

PracticeTwo species compete: x˙=x(3−2x−y), y˙=y(2−x−y). Find all fixed points, classify them, and say whether the species can coexist.›

The idea in plain words. Same method as rabbits vs sheep (§6), but here each species hurts itself more than it hurts the other, so the coexistence point comes out stable instead of a saddle.

  1. Fixed points: x=0 or 2x+y=3; y=0 or x+y=2. Gives (0,0), (0,2), (3/2,0), and 2x+y=3, x+y=2⇒x=1,y=1.
  2. J=(3−4x−y−x−y2−x−2y).
  3. (0,0): (3002), unstable node.
  4. (3/2,0): (−3−1.500.5), eigenvalues −3,0.5: saddle.
  5. (0,2): (10−2−2), eigenvalues 1,−2: saddle.
  6. (1,1): (−2−1−1−1), τ=−3, Δ=2−1=1, τ2−4Δ=5>0: stable node, λ=−3±52=−0.38,−2.62.

Final answer: the coexistence point (1,1) is a stable node, so both species survive together; the single-species states are saddles.
