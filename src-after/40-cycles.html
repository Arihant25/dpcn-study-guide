9Limit cycles and the Hopf bifurcation

Deck 12 · slides 25–33

A heartbeat, a body-temperature rhythm or a firing neuron oscillates by itself, with no outside push, and returns to the same rhythm after a disturbance. Such a self-sustained oscillation is a limit cycle: a closed loop in the phase plane that nearby paths are pulled onto. The standard example (called the Stuart–Landau oscillator) becomes easy once you switch to polar coordinates, and its birth as a parameter changes is the Hopf bifurcation.

What the exam can ask
  • Convert \(\dot z = (\mu + i\omega - |z|^2)z\) to polar form and find the limit cycle. (2024 mid-sem Q2, 7 marks)
  • Write the Cartesian form, the Jacobian at the origin, and its eigenvalues.
  • Explain the bifurcation at \(\mu = 0\) (supercritical Hopf), and the difference from subcritical.

What a limit cycle is

A limit cycle is an isolated closed path: the paths next to it are not closed themselves but spiral towards it or away from it. That is the difference from a center (§2), where every nearby path is its own closed loop. A limit cycle is stable if paths on both sides spiral onto it, unstable if they spiral away, and half-stable if one side approaches and the other leaves.

The model

\[\dot z = (\mu + i\omega - |z|^2)\,z, \qquad z = x + iy\]

\(z\) is the point \((x, y)\) written as one complex number (M3). \(\mu\) (Greek "mu") is the parameter we will vary. \(\omega\) (Greek "omega", written \(w\) on the slides) sets the speed of rotation. \(|z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\) is the distance from the origin.

Derivation to learn · polar form and the limit cycle
  1. Polar form. Write \(z = re^{i\theta}\), so \(|z| = r\).
  2. Left side. By the product rule (M3): \(\dot z = (\dot r + ir\dot\theta)\,e^{i\theta}\).
  3. Right side. \((\mu + i\omega - r^2)\,re^{i\theta}\).
  4. Cancel \(e^{i\theta}\) (never zero) and compare: \(\dot r + ir\dot\theta = (\mu - r^2)r + i\omega r\).
  5. Match real parts and imaginary parts separately (two complex numbers are equal only if both parts are equal): \[\dot r = (\mu - r^2)\,r = \mu r - r^3, \qquad \dot\theta = \omega .\]
  6. The angle just increases at constant speed \(\omega\): the point always turns around the origin.
  7. The distance obeys a 1-D equation. Fixed points: \(r^* = 0\), and \(r^* = \sqrt\mu\) when \(\mu > 0\) (a distance cannot be negative). Slope: \(f'(r) = \mu - 3r^2\). So \(f'(0) = \mu\) and \(f'(\sqrt\mu) = \mu - 3\mu = -2\mu\).
  8. \(\mu < 0\): only \(r^* = 0\), with \(f'(0) = \mu < 0\): stable. Every path spirals into the origin.
  9. \(\mu > 0\): \(r^* = 0\) is unstable (\(f'(0) = \mu > 0\)) and \(r^* = \sqrt\mu\) is stable (\(f'(\sqrt\mu) = -2\mu < 0\)). Every path (except the origin itself) approaches the circle of radius \(\sqrt\mu\) and goes round it at speed \(\omega\). That circle is a stable limit cycle.

Notice that \(\dot r = \mu r - r^3\) is exactly the supercritical pitchfork from the Mid-sem guide (§9). The slides point this out with a double arrow. The difference is that \(r\) cannot be negative, so the two prongs \(\pm\sqrt\mu\) of the pitchfork become one circle.

The same system in \(x, y\), and its Jacobian

Multiplying out \((\mu + i\omega - (x^2 + y^2))(x + iy)\) and separating real and imaginary parts:

\[\dot x = \mu x - \omega y - x(x^2 + y^2), \qquad \dot y = \omega x + \mu y - y(x^2 + y^2).\]
Derivation to learn · the origin is an unstable spiral (slides 30–31, \(\mu = 1\))
  1. With \(\mu = 1\): \(f_1 = x - \omega y - x^3 - xy^2\), \(f_2 = \omega x + y - x^2y - y^3\).
  2. Jacobian: \[J = \begin{pmatrix}1 - 3x^2 - y^2 & -\omega - 2xy\\ \omega - 2xy & 1 - x^2 - 3y^2\end{pmatrix}.\]
  3. At \((0,0)\): \(J = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -\omega\\ \omega & 1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\tau = 2\), \(\Delta = 1 + \omega^2\).
  4. \(\lambda = \frac{2\pm\sqrt{4 - 4(1 + \omega^2)}}{2} = 1\pm i\omega\). Positive real part with rotation: unstable spiral.
  5. So paths starting near the origin spiral away from it, consistent with \(\dot r = r - r^3 > 0\) for small \(r\). They end on the circle \(r = 1\).

The Hopf bifurcation

As \(\mu\) passes 0, the eigenvalues at the origin, \(\mu\pm i\omega\), cross from the left half (negative real part) to the right half of the complex plane, and a limit cycle is born. A stable point turning into a stable oscillation this way is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation. The cycle's radius grows like \(\sqrt\mu\).

The slides also give the general form \(\dot z = (a + i\omega + b|z|^2)z\) with \(b = \alpha + i\beta\). In polar form \(\dot r = (a + \alpha r^2)r\).

  • \(\alpha < 0\): supercritical. For \(a > 0\) a stable cycle of radius \(\sqrt{-a/\alpha}\) exists around an unstable origin. (Our model is \(\alpha = -1\).)
  • \(\alpha > 0\): subcritical. For \(a < 0\) an unstable cycle surrounds a stable origin; as \(a\) passes 0 the cycle shrinks onto the origin and disappears, and for \(a > 0\) paths spiral out with nothing nearby to stop them. This is the 2-D version of the subcritical pitchfork: a sudden jump.
What to remember
  • \(z = re^{i\theta}\): \(\dot z = (\mu + i\omega - |z|^2)z\) becomes \(\dot r = \mu r - r^3\), \(\dot\theta = \omega\).
  • \(\mu > 0\): stable limit cycle \(r = \sqrt\mu\), unstable origin. \(\mu < 0\): stable origin.
  • Jacobian at the origin: eigenvalues \(\mu\pm i\omega\). Supercritical Hopf at \(\mu = 0\).

10Van der Pol, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, and networks of oscillators

Deck 12 · slides 34–43

Most limit cycles are not perfect circles and cannot be found with a formula; they are found by simulation. This section covers the classic example (van der Pol), a theorem that proves a limit cycle exists without finding it (Poincaré–Bendixson), and what happens when many oscillators are coupled on a network and some of them stop working.

The van der Pol oscillator

\[\ddot x + \mu(x^2 - 1)\dot x + x = 0\]

\(\ddot x\) is the acceleration (slope of the slope). Without the middle term this is a simple spring, \(\ddot x = -x\). The middle term is a "friction" that changes sign: for small \(|x| < 1\) it is negative (it pumps energy in, so small swings grow), and for large \(|x| > 1\) it is positive (it removes energy, so large swings shrink). The balance is a limit cycle. Written as two first-order equations with \(v = \dot x\): \(\dot x = v\), \(\dot v = -x - \mu(x^2 - 1)v\).

The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

Statement

If a path stays forever inside a closed, bounded region of the plane that contains no fixed points, then the path must approach a closed orbit.

How to use it: build a trapping region, a ring-shaped area such that on both its inner and outer edges the flow points into the ring. Then every path inside stays inside. If there is no fixed point in the ring, the theorem guarantees a closed orbit in it.

Worked example · the model of §9 with \(\mu = 1\)

Take the ring \(0.5 \le r \le 2\). On the inner edge \(r = 0.5\): \(\dot r = 0.5 - 0.125 = 0.375 > 0\), the flow points outwards, into the ring. On the outer edge \(r = 2\): \(\dot r = 2 - 8 = -6 < 0\), the flow points inwards, into the ring. The only fixed point is the origin, which is outside the ring. So by Poincaré–Bendixson there is a closed orbit in the ring. (We know it is \(r = 1\).)

The theorem works only in two dimensions. In three dimensions paths can wander forever without settling, which is how chaos becomes possible (§14).

Networks of oscillators that "age" (slides 38–43)

The slides couple \(N\) Stuart–Landau oscillators all-to-all:

\[\dot z_i = (\mu_i + i\omega - |z_i|^2)z_i + \frac KN\sum_{k=1}^{N}(z_k - z_i).\]

A fraction \(p\) of the oscillators are "inactive" (\(\mu < 0\): alone they would stop at 0), the rest "active" (\(\mu > 0\): alone they oscillate). The collective oscillation is measured by the order parameter \(Z = \frac1N\sum_jz_j\), and \(Q = |Z(p)|/|Z(0)|\) compares it with the case of no inactive oscillators. The simulation on the slides (\(N = 50\)) shows \(Q\) falling as \(p\) grows; with strong coupling (\(K = 30\)) it drops to exactly 0 at about \(p \approx 0.7\): the whole network stops oscillating. This is the aging transition (Daido and Nakanishi, 2004). Related papers on the slides show that in networks with hubs, losing low-degree nodes can kill the collective oscillation (Tanaka et al., 2012), and that targeted interventions control epidemics in patch networks better than random ones (Tanaka et al., 2014).

The plot on slide 41 labels \(\mu_a = -0.5\), \(\mu_{ia} = 1\); by slide 40's definitions these look swapped (inactive should be negative).

What to remember
  • Van der Pol: \(\ddot x + \mu(x^2 - 1)\dot x + x = 0\); negative damping for small \(x\), positive for large \(x\), limit cycle in between.
  • Poincaré–Bendixson: trapping region with no fixed point ⇒ closed orbit. Only in 2-D.
  • Aging transition: enough inactive oscillators stop the whole network.

ConceptWhy can there be no chaos in a 2-D system?›

By Poincaré–Bendixson, a path trapped in a bounded region of the plane must approach a fixed point or a closed orbit. It cannot wander irregularly forever. Chaos needs at least three dimensions (like Lorenz) or a map.
