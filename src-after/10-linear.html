Part 12-D linear systems

1Two variables at once · straight-line solutions

Deck 11 · slides 4–15

In the first half, one quantity \(x\) changed over time. Now two quantities change together, \(x\) and \(y\), and each one's rate depends on both. The simplest case is a linear system, where each rate is just a weighted sum of \(x\) and \(y\). Such a system can be solved exactly, and the solution is built from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix.

What the exam can ask
  • Show that \(\mathbf x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\) solves \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\) when \(\mathbf v\) is an eigenvector, and derive \(\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0\).
  • Write the general solution.
  • For \(\dot x = ax,\ \dot y = -y\), describe what happens for different values of \(a\) (node, star, line of fixed points, saddle).

The system

\[\dot x = ax + by, \qquad \dot y = cx + dy \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x,\quad A = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix},\quad \mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix}x\\ y\end{pmatrix}.\]

\(a, b, c, d\) are fixed numbers. \(\mathbf x\) is the point \((x, y)\). The plane of all possible points is called the phase plane, and a drawing of how points move in it is a phase portrait. The origin \((0,0)\) is always a fixed point, because there both rates are 0.

A first example with no coupling

Take \(b = c = 0\) and \(d = -1\): \(\dot x = ax\), \(\dot y = -y\). Each equation involves only one variable, so each is the basic equation \(\dot y = ay\) from the maths primer (Mid-sem M8):

\[x(t) = x_0e^{at}, \qquad y(t) = y_0e^{-t}.\]

\(y\) always shrinks. What \(x\) does depends on \(a\), and that gives five pictures, all on the slides.

In the node case, which direction is "slower"? For \(a < -1\), \(x\) shrinks faster than \(y\) (rate \(|a| > 1\)), so the \(x\)-part disappears first and paths come into the origin along the \(y\)-axis. For \(-1 < a < 0\) it is the other way round: they come in along the \(x\)-axis.

The general case · looking for straight-line solutions

In the uncoupled example, a point on an axis stays on that axis and just slides in or out. Those are straight-line solutions. The idea for any matrix \(A\) is to find the straight lines that the motion keeps, which turn out to be the eigenvectors.

Derivation to learn · eigen-solutions and the characteristic equation
  1. Guess a solution that stays on one line and grows or shrinks exponentially: \(\mathbf x(t) = e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\), where \(\mathbf v\) is a fixed vector and \(\lambda\) a fixed number.
  2. Put it into the equation. Left side: \(\dot{\mathbf x} = \lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\) (slope of \(e^{\lambda t}\) is \(\lambda e^{\lambda t}\)). Right side: \(A\mathbf x = e^{\lambda t}A\mathbf v\). So \(\lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v = e^{\lambda t}A\mathbf v\).
  3. Cancel \(e^{\lambda t}\), which is never zero: \(A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\). So the guess works exactly when \(\mathbf v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) and \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue. This is called an eigen-solution.
  4. Find \(\lambda\). \(A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\) with \(\mathbf v\neq0\) needs \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\): \[\det\begin{pmatrix}a - \lambda & b\\ c & d - \lambda\end{pmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = 0.\]
  5. Multiply out: \(\lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc) = 0\), that is \[\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0, \qquad \tau = a + d,\quad \Delta = ad - bc.\]
  6. Solve: \(\lambda_{1,2} = \dfrac{\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}\).
  7. General solution. The equation is linear, so adding two solutions gives another solution: \[\mathbf x(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2,\] where the numbers \(c_1, c_2\) are fixed by the starting point.

Reading the general solution: each part grows if its \(\lambda\) is positive and shrinks if negative. After a long time the part with the larger \(\lambda\) dominates. That single observation gives every picture in §2.

What to remember
  • \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\). Try \(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\) and get \(A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\).
  • \(\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0\), \(\tau = a + d\), \(\Delta = ad - bc\).
  • \(\mathbf x(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}\mathbf v_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}\mathbf v_2\).
  • \(\dot x = ax,\ \dot y = -y\): node (\(a<0,\ a\ne-1\)), star (\(a = -1\)), line of fixed points (\(a = 0\)), saddle (\(a > 0\)).

Guess \(e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v\); substitute to get \(\lambda e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v = e^{\lambda t}A\mathbf v\); cancel to get \(A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v\); non-zero \(\mathbf v\) needs \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\); expand \((a-\lambda)(d-\lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad - bc)\).

ConceptFor \(\dot x = -3x,\ \dot y = -y\), along which axis do paths enter the origin?›

\(x\) shrinks like \(e^{-3t}\), faster than \(y\) (\(e^{-t}\)). The \(x\)-part vanishes first, so paths come in along the \(y\)-axis. It is a stable node.

2Classifying a fixed point · saddles, nodes, spirals, centers

Deck 11 · slides 25–28 · Deck 12 · slides 2–5

Every 2-D linear system has the origin as a fixed point, and the shape of the motion around it falls into a few types. You can tell which type from just two numbers, the trace \(\tau\) and the determinant \(\Delta\), without finding any eigenvectors.

What the exam can ask
  • Classify the fixed point of a given matrix (saddle, node, spiral, center) and say whether it is stable.
  • Draw or explain the trace–determinant diagram.

The rules, and why

Everything follows from the eigenvalue formula \(\lambda = \frac{\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}{2}\), together with two facts: \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta\) and \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \tau\).

ConditionEigenvaluesTypeWhy
\(\Delta < 0\)real, one positive, one negativesaddle (always unstable)The product \(\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta\) is negative, so they have opposite signs: one direction grows, one shrinks.
\(0 < \Delta < \tau^2/4\)real, same signnode: stable if \(\tau < 0\), unstable if \(\tau > 0\)Positive product means same sign; the sum \(\tau\) tells you which sign.
\(\Delta > \tau^2/4\)complex pair \(\frac\tau2\pm i\,\cdot\)spiral: stable if \(\tau < 0\), unstable if \(\tau > 0\)\(\tau^2 - 4\Delta < 0\), so the square root is imaginary: rotation, with real part \(\tau/2\) deciding growth or decay (M3).
\(\tau = 0,\ \Delta > 0\)\(\pm i\sqrt\Delta\)centerPure rotation with no growth or decay: closed loops around the origin.
\(\tau^2 = 4\Delta\)repeatedstar or degenerate nodeThe borderline between nodes and spirals.
A slide statement to avoid

The slides say a spiral "rotates counterclockwise for \(a > d\) and clockwise for \(a < d\)". That rule comes from a book that writes the matrix differently, and it is wrong for \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\) with \(A = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ c & d\end{pmatrix}\). The correct test: look at \(c\). If \(c > 0\) the rotation is counterclockwise, if \(c < 0\) it is clockwise. (At the point \((1, 0)\), \(\dot y = c\), so a positive \(c\) pushes the point upwards, which is counterclockwise.)

Derivation to learn · classifying a fixed point
  1. Write \(A\), compute \(\tau = a + d\) and \(\Delta = ad - bc\).
  2. If \(\Delta < 0\): saddle. Stop.
  3. Otherwise compute \(\tau^2 - 4\Delta\). If negative: spiral (or center if \(\tau = 0\)). If positive: node.
  4. Stability from the sign of \(\tau\): negative means stable, positive means unstable.
  5. Back it up by writing the eigenvalues \(\lambda = \frac{\tau\pm\sqrt{\tau^2-4\Delta}}{2}\).

What to remember
  • \(\Delta < 0\): saddle. \(\Delta > 0\): node if \(\tau^2 > 4\Delta\), spiral if \(\tau^2 < 4\Delta\); stable if \(\tau < 0\). \(\tau = 0,\ \Delta > 0\): center.
  • Rotation direction: counterclockwise if \(c > 0\).

ConceptWhy can a saddle never be stable?›

\(\Delta = \lambda_1\lambda_2 < 0\) means one eigenvalue is positive. The part of the solution along that eigenvector grows like \(e^{\lambda t}\), so almost every starting point is pushed away.

3Solving an initial value problem

Deck 11 · slides 16–19

An initial value problem gives the equations and a starting point, and asks for the exact formulas \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\). The method: find eigenvalues and eigenvectors, write the general solution, then choose \(c_1, c_2\) so it starts at the given point. This is the worked example on the slides.

Worked derivation · \(\dot x = x + y,\ \dot y = 4x - 2y\), starting at \((2, -3)\)
  1. Matrix form. \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 4 & -2\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\tau = -1\), \(\Delta = (1)(-2) - (1)(4) = -6\).
  2. Eigenvalues. \(\lambda^2 + \lambda - 6 = 0\Rightarrow(\lambda - 2)(\lambda + 3) = 0\Rightarrow\lambda_1 = 2,\ \lambda_2 = -3\).
  3. Eigenvectors. \(\lambda = 2\): first row of \(A - 2I\) gives \(-v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(\mathbf v_1 = (1, 1)\). \(\lambda = -3\): first row of \(A + 3I\) gives \(4v_1 + v_2 = 0\), so \(\mathbf v_2 = (1, -4)\).
  4. General solution. \(\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = c_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}e^{2t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-4\end{pmatrix}e^{-3t}\).
  5. Use the starting point. At \(t = 0\), \(e^0 = 1\): \(x(0) = c_1 + c_2 = 2\) and \(y(0) = c_1 - 4c_2 = -3\). Subtract the second from the first: \(5c_2 = 5\), so \(c_2 = 1\), and then \(c_1 = 1\).
  6. Answer. \[x(t) = e^{2t} + e^{-3t}, \qquad y(t) = e^{2t} - 4e^{-3t}.\]
  7. Check. At \(t = 0\): \(x = 2\), \(y = 1 - 4 = -3\). Correct. And \(\dot x = 2e^{2t} - 3e^{-3t}\), while \(x + y = 2e^{2t} - 3e^{-3t}\). Correct.

What it means: \(\Delta < 0\), so the origin is a saddle (picture in §1). The \(e^{-3t}\) part dies away and the \(e^{2t}\) part takes over, so the point ends up moving out along the line \(y = x\) (the direction \(\mathbf v_1\)). The slide's red note asks what happens if you start at \((1, -4)\) instead. That point is exactly on \(\mathbf v_2\), so \(c_1 = 0\), \(c_2 = 1\), and the solution \((e^{-3t}, -4e^{-3t})\) slides straight into the origin, the only way to reach a saddle.

What to remember

Eigenvalues → eigenvectors → general solution → two equations for \(c_1, c_2\) from \(t = 0\) → check by substituting back.

Check yourself
CalcSolve \(\dot x = x + y,\ \dot y = 4x - 2y\) starting at \((0, 5)\).›

\(c_1 + c_2 = 0\) and \(c_1 - 4c_2 = 5\). Subtract: \(5c_2 = -5\), \(c_2 = -1\), \(c_1 = 1\). So \(x = e^{2t} - e^{-3t}\), \(y = e^{2t} + 4e^{-3t}\). Check at 0: \(x = 0\), \(y = 5\).

4Romeo and Juliet · a linear system as a story

Deck 11 · slides 21–29

The slides use a love story to practise classification. \(R(t)\) is Romeo's love for Juliet and \(J(t)\) is Juliet's love for Romeo (positive means love, negative means hate). Each person's feelings change according to their own feelings and the other's. Classifying the fixed point \(R = J = 0\) (mutual indifference) tells you how the romance goes.

The fickle Juliet model

Romeo echoes Juliet: he warms up when she loves him (\(\dot R = bJ\), \(b > 0\)). Juliet runs away when he loves her and is attracted when he backs off (\(\dot J = -cR\), \(c > 0\)).

Derivation to learn · the center
  1. \(A = \begin{pmatrix}0 & b\\ -c & 0\end{pmatrix}\).
  2. \(\tau = 0 + 0 = 0\). \(\Delta = 0\cdot0 - b(-c) = bc > 0\).
  3. \(\lambda^2 + bc = 0\Rightarrow\lambda = \pm i\sqrt{bc}\): purely imaginary.
  4. So the origin is a center: closed loops. Love and hate cycle forever. They love each other at the same time for about a quarter of each cycle (when both \(R\) and \(J\) are positive).

(The slide writes \(\dot J = -CR\) with a capital \(C\); it is the same constant \(c\). Since \(-c < 0\), the loops run clockwise in the \((R, J)\) plane.)

The general model and the slide's scenario

The general version is \(\dot R = aR + bJ\), \(\dot J = cR + dJ\). \(a\) and \(d\) say how each person reacts to their own feelings (enthusiasm if positive, caution if negative); \(b\) and \(c\) say how they react to the other's.

Worked example · slide 29

Romeo is not afraid to love (\(a = 0.1 > 0\)) and is an enthusiastic lover (\(b = 1\)); Juliet is cautious (\(c = -1\), \(d = 0\)).

  1. \(A = \begin{pmatrix}0.1 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\tau = 0.1\), \(\Delta = 0 - (1)(-1) = 1\).
  2. \(\tau^2 - 4\Delta = 0.01 - 4 = -3.99 < 0\): a spiral. \(\tau > 0\): unstable.
  3. \(\lambda = 0.05\pm i\frac{\sqrt{3.99}}{2}\approx0.05\pm0.999i\).

An unstable spiral: the cycles of love and hate grow bigger and bigger over time.

Check yourself
CalcRomeo and Juliet are both cautious (\(a = d = -1\)) and both respond to each other's love (\(b = c = 2\)). What happens?›

\(A = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 2\\ 2 & -1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\tau = -2\), \(\Delta = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0\): a saddle, \(\lambda = 1\) and \(-3\). Their feelings run away, either to ever-growing mutual love or ever-growing mutual hate, depending on where they start.
