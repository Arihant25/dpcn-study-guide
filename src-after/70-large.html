17Will a large system be stable?

Deck 13 · slides 50–71

An ecosystem has hundreds of species, each affecting many others. Writing down every interaction is impossible. In 1972 Robert May asked a different question: if the interactions were random, how likely is the whole system to be stable? His answer, using a result about the eigenvalues of large random matrices, is that too many species, too many connections or too-strong interactions make stability impossible.

What the exam can ask
  • Define the community matrix and explain the stability condition on its eigenvalues.
  • Derive the Jacobian of the generalised Lotka–Volterra model, \(M = D(x^*)A\).
  • State and explain May's criterion \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2} < d\), and the effect of predator–prey vs competition/mutualism pairs.

The community matrix

For many species, \(\dot x_i = f_i(\mathbf x)\). An equilibrium \(\mathbf x^*\) has \(f_i(\mathbf x^*) = 0\) for every species. Exactly as in §5, a small push \(\Delta\mathbf x\) obeys \(\frac{d\Delta\mathbf x}{dt}\approx M\Delta\mathbf x\), where \(M = J|_{\mathbf x^*}\) is the Jacobian at the equilibrium, called the community matrix. Its entry \(M_{ij}\) says how a small increase of species \(j\) changes the growth of species \(i\). The solution is \(\Delta\mathbf x(t) = e^{Mt}\Delta\mathbf x(0)\), a combination of \(e^{\lambda t}\) terms over the eigenvalues of \(M\). Stability depends on the eigenvalue furthest to the right: stable if its real part is negative.

Slide 52 writes the expansion as \(f(\Delta x(0))\); it should be \(f(\mathbf x^* + \Delta\mathbf x(0))\). Slide 54 writes \(\Delta x(0)e^{Mt}\); the matrix should come first, \(e^{Mt}\Delta\mathbf x(0)\).

Derivation to learn · the GLV community matrix
  1. Generalised Lotka–Volterra model: \(\dot x_i = x_i\Big(r_i + \sum_ja_{ij}x_j\Big)\), or in matrix form \(\dot{\mathbf x} = D(\mathbf x)(\mathbf r + A\mathbf x)\), where \(D(\mathbf x)\) is the diagonal matrix with \(\mathbf x\) on the diagonal, \(r_i\) the growth rate of species \(i\) alone, and \(a_{ij}\) the effect of species \(j\) on species \(i\).
  2. Off-diagonal entries (\(j\ne i\)): only the term \(x_ia_{ij}x_j\) contains \(x_j\), so \(\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j} = a_{ij}x_i\).
  3. Diagonal entries: by the product rule, \(\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_i} = \Big(r_i + \sum_ja_{ij}x_j\Big) + a_{ii}x_i\).
  4. At an equilibrium where every species is present, the bracket is zero (that is what \(\dot x_i = 0\) with \(x_i\ne0\) means). So every entry is \(M_{ij} = a_{ij}x_i^*\): \[M = D(\mathbf x^*)\,A.\]

May's random matrix and his criterion

May skipped the details and built the community matrix at random, for \(S\) species:

  • Each off-diagonal entry is 0 with probability \(1 - C\) (species do not interact), and with probability \(C\) it is a random number with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\). \(C\) is the connectance, the fraction of possible interactions that exist.
  • Each diagonal entry is \(-d\) (self-regulation, like a carrying capacity).
Derivation to learn · May's criterion
  1. Circular law (a theorem about large random matrices): if an \(S\times S\) matrix has independent entries with mean 0 and variance 1, the eigenvalues of the matrix divided by \(\sqrt S\) fill a disc of radius 1 in the complex plane, evenly.
  2. May's off-diagonal entries have mean 0 and variance \(C\sigma^2\) (zero with probability \(1 - C\), variance \(\sigma^2\) otherwise). Dividing them by \(\sqrt{C\sigma^2}\) gives variance 1.
  3. So by the circular law, the off-diagonal part has eigenvalues in a disc of radius \(\sqrt{S}\sqrt{C\sigma^2} = \sqrt{SC\sigma^2}\), centred at 0.
  4. Adding \(-d\) on the diagonal shifts every eigenvalue left by \(d\): the disc is now centred at \(-d\).
  5. The rightmost eigenvalue is at about \(-d + \sqrt{SC\sigma^2}\). Stability needs this to be negative: \[\sqrt{SC\sigma^2} < d.\]

Meaning: more species \(S\), more connections \(C\) or stronger interactions \(\sigma\) all make the disc bigger, and past a point it crosses into the unstable right half. Complexity destabilises, unless self-regulation \(d\) is strong.

Numbers

\(S = 100\) species, \(C = 0.1\), \(\sigma = 0.5\), \(d = 1\). \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2} = \sqrt{100\times0.1\times0.25} = \sqrt{2.5} = 1.58 > 1\): unstable. With \(C = 0.03\): \(\sqrt{0.75} = 0.87 < 1\): stable.

Slide 61 writes the circular-law conditions as \(\mathbb E[X_{ij}] = 0\) and \(\mathbb E[X_{ij}] = 1\); the second should be \(\mathbb E[X_{ij}^2] = 1\) (unit variance).

The elliptic law · interaction types matter (Allesina and Tang, 2012)

If the pair \((M_{ij}, M_{ji})\) is correlated with correlation \(\rho\), the disc becomes an ellipse with horizontal half-width \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2}(1 + \rho)\), and the criterion becomes \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2}(1 + \rho) < d\).

  • Predator–prey pairs (one positive, one negative, so \(\rho < 0\)): the ellipse is tall and narrow, stretched along the imaginary axis. The criterion is easier to meet: predator–prey interactions stabilise.
  • Competition or mutualism pairs (same signs, \(\rho > 0\)): the ellipse is wide and flat, reaching further right. Harder to meet: these interactions destabilise.

The slide formulas \(\sqrt{SC} < \theta/(1\mp\mathbb E^2(|X|)/\sigma^2)\) are the same criterion with \(\theta = d/\sigma\) and \(\rho = \mp\mathbb E^2(|X|)/\sigma^2\).

Real networks are not random (Meena et al., 2023)

The last paper on the slides argues that Jacobians of real network dynamics are not random: their entries scale with node degrees, \(J_{ii}\sim -C\,d_i^{\mu}\) and \(J_{ij}\sim d_i^{\nu}A_{ij}d_j^{\rho}\), with exponents set by the dynamics (epidemic, gene regulation, population, and so on). A single number built from these exponents and the network's degree distribution sorts systems into three classes: always unstable, sensitive (stable only for some parameters), and stable, where large, heterogeneous networks are guaranteed stable. So scale and hubs can help stability, the opposite of May's random-matrix picture.

What to remember
  • Community matrix \(M = J|_{\mathbf x^*}\); stable iff rightmost eigenvalue has negative real part.
  • GLV: \(\dot{\mathbf x} = D(\mathbf x)(\mathbf r + A\mathbf x)\), \(M = D(\mathbf x^*)A\).
  • May: stable iff \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2} < d\). Elliptic law: \(\sqrt{SC\sigma^2}(1 + \rho) < d\); predator–prey (\(\rho<0\)) stabilises.
Check yourself
Calc\(S = 400\), \(\sigma = 0.1\), \(d = 1\). What is the largest connectance C that keeps the random system stable?›

Need \(\sqrt{400\times C\times0.01} < 1\), i.e. \(\sqrt{4C} < 1\), \(C < 0.25\).

18The double pendulum

Deck 13 · slides 73–79

A double pendulum is one pendulum hanging from the end of another. For large swings it is a classic chaotic system. For small swings it behaves simply: its motion is a mix of two "normal modes", one where the two parts swing together and one where they swing opposite each other. The slides derive these from the energy (the Lagrangian method).

Setting up

Two rods of length \(L_1, L_2\) with masses \(m_1, m_2\) at their ends. \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_2\) are the angles of the rods from the vertical. \(g\) is the acceleration due to gravity.

  • Positions: \(x_1 = L_1\sin\phi_1\), \(y_1 = -L_1\cos\phi_1\), \(x_2 = x_1 + L_2\sin\phi_2\), \(y_2 = y_1 - L_2\cos\phi_2\).
  • Kinetic energy \(T = \frac12(m_1 + m_2)L_1^2\dot\phi_1^2 + \frac{m_2}2L_2^2\dot\phi_2^2 + m_2L_1L_2\dot\phi_1\dot\phi_2\cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2)\).
  • Potential energy \(V = -(m_1 + m_2)gL_1\cos\phi_1 - m_2gL_2\cos\phi_2\).
  • The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L = T - V\), and the equations of motion come from \(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\phi_i} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi_i} = 0\).
Derivation to learn · small swings and normal modes
  1. Small angles. Use \(\cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2)\approx1\) in \(T\) and \(\cos\phi\approx1 - \frac{\phi^2}{2}\) in \(V\). The equations become linear: \[(m_1 + m_2)L_1^2\ddot\phi_1 + m_2L_1L_2\ddot\phi_2 + (m_1 + m_2)gL_1\phi_1 = 0,\] \[m_2L_1L_2\ddot\phi_1 + m_2L_2^2\ddot\phi_2 + m_2gL_2\phi_2 = 0.\]
  2. Matrix form. \(M\ddot{\boldsymbol\phi} = -K\boldsymbol\phi\) with \(M = \begin{pmatrix}(m_1+m_2)L_1^2 & m_2L_1L_2\\ m_2L_1L_2 & m_2L_2^2\end{pmatrix}\), \(K = \begin{pmatrix}(m_1+m_2)gL_1 & 0\\ 0 & m_2gL_2\end{pmatrix}\).
  3. Try an oscillation \(\boldsymbol\phi = \mathbf He^{i\omega t}\): then \(\ddot{\boldsymbol\phi} = -\omega^2\boldsymbol\phi\), so \((K - \omega^2M)\mathbf H = 0\), which needs \(\det(K - \omega^2M) = 0\).
  4. Equal lengths \(L_1 = L_2 = L\), mass ratio \(\mu = m_2/m_1\). Writing \(\lambda = \omega^2L/g\), the determinant condition becomes \((1 + \mu)(1 - \lambda)^2 = \mu\lambda^2\), with solutions \(\lambda = 1 + \mu\pm\sqrt{\mu(1 + \mu)}\). So \[\omega_{1,2}^2 = \frac gL\Big[1 + \mu\pm\sqrt{(1 + \mu)\mu}\Big].\]
  5. Equal masses (\(\mu = 1\)): \(\omega_{1,2} = \sqrt{\frac gL}\sqrt{2\pm\sqrt2}\).
  6. Mode shapes. The fast mode \(\omega_1\) has \(\mathbf H = \big(1,\ -\sqrt{(1+\mu)/\mu}\big)\): the two rods swing in opposite directions. The slow mode \(\omega_2\) has \(\big(1,\ +\sqrt{(1+\mu)/\mu}\big)\): they swing together. The general small motion is \(A_1\mathbf H_1\cos(\omega_1t + \alpha_1) + A_2\mathbf H_2\cos(\omega_2t + \alpha_2)\).
With the slide's starting condition · \(\phi_1(0) = 0\), \(\phi_2(0) = \pi/6\), both at rest
  1. At rest, so both phases \(\alpha = 0\). Let \(s = \sqrt{(1+\mu)/\mu}\).
  2. \(\phi_1(0) = A_1 + A_2 = 0\), so \(A_2 = -A_1\).
  3. \(\phi_2(0) = -sA_1 + sA_2 = -2sA_1 = \pi/6\), so \(A_1 = -\frac{\pi}{12}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}}\) and \(A_2 = +\frac{\pi}{12}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}}\).
  4. Equal masses, \(L = 0.25\) m: \(\sqrt{g/L} = 6.26\), \(\omega_1 = 11.6\), \(\omega_2 = 4.8\) rad/s. In degrees, \(\phi_2(t) = 15°(\cos\omega_1t + \cos\omega_2t)\) and \(\phi_1(t) = \frac{15°}{\sqrt2}(\cos\omega_2t - \cos\omega_1t)\).
Slide errors

Slide 77 writes the eigenvector entry as \(\mp\frac{\sqrt{1+\mu}}{\mu}\); it should be \(\mp\sqrt{\frac{1+\mu}{\mu}}\) (the two agree only for \(\mu = 1\)). It also writes both modes with \(A_1\) and the same phase \(\alpha\), and gives \(A_2\) with a minus sign; \(A_2 = -A_1 = +\frac{\pi}{12}\sqrt{\mu/(1+\mu)}\).

For large swings the small-angle steps fail, the equations stay nonlinear, and the motion becomes chaotic: tiny changes in the starting angles lead to completely different motion.

What to remember
  • Small angles: \(M\ddot{\boldsymbol\phi} = -K\boldsymbol\phi\), \(\det(K - \omega^2M) = 0\).
  • \(\omega_{1,2}^2 = \frac gL[1 + \mu\pm\sqrt{\mu(1+\mu)}]\); equal masses \(\sqrt{g/L}\sqrt{2\pm\sqrt2}\).
  • Fast mode: opposite swings. Slow mode: together.
