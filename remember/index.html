How to use this page

You need both. The formulas are what you start from and what you box at the end. The proofs are where the marks are, and the 2026 mid-sem gave 0 for a formula written without its derivation.

  1. Learn the formulas by heart. They're short lists, one per page.
  2. Then read the two questions and the recipe. Most proofs follow one of them, so you have far fewer separate things to learn than it looks.
  3. Learn each proof in two layers. First its one-line idea (the shaded line). Then the steps, each with its reason after "∵". If you remember the idea, you can usually rebuild the steps.
  4. Cover the proof and write it on paper. Then check it against the page. Writing it out is what makes it stick, not rereading.
  5. Use the flashcards. "Facts" drills the formulas, "Proofs" drills the derivations. Cards you miss come back later in the round.

A few proofs a day for a week beats all of them the night before. (Annoying, but true.)

Formulas to remember

Every formula the course uses, grouped by page. Know these by heart, because you need them to start a proof and to box its answer. Under each formula, "where" says what every letter in it means. Each one is proved further down in Every proof, the short way.

Symbols used everywhere
  • x˙ (x dot)How fast x changes with time, dx/dt. Two dots, x¨, is how fast x˙ changes (acceleration).
  • x∗ (x star)A fixed point: a value where nothing changes any more (x˙=0, or xn+1=xn for a map).
  • f′(x)The slope (derivative) of f at x. In x˙=f(x), f is the rule that gives the rate of change.
  • ∂f/∂xPartial derivative: the slope of f as x changes, holding the other variables fixed.
  • λ (lambda)An eigenvalue. Near a fixed point, a small push grows or shrinks like eλt, so negative means it dies out (stable).
  • i−1. Also eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ, a point at angle θ on the unit circle.
  • ⟨ ⟩"The average of". ⟨k⟩ is the average degree, ⟨k2⟩ the average of the squared degrees.
  • iff"If and only if": the condition works both ways.

Page 1 · Network basics (classes 1 to 6)

  • Degree of node iki=∑jAijwhere A is the adjacency matrix: Aij=1 if nodes i and j are linked, 0 if not. ki (the degree) is how many links node i has, so it's the sum of row i.
  • Handshaking lemma∑iki=2L, so ⟨k⟩=2L/Nwhere L is the number of links, N the number of nodes, and ⟨k⟩ the average degree.
  • Walks[An]ij = number of walks of length n from i to jwhere An is A multiplied by itself n times, and [An]ij is its entry in row i, column j. A walk of length n is n steps along links (repeats allowed).
  • Triangles16tr⁡(A3)where tr⁡ (trace) means the sum of the diagonal entries.
  • Euler paths0 odd-degree nodes gives a circuit, 2 gives a trail, more gives nonewhere an Euler trail uses every link exactly once, and a circuit is a trail that ends where it started.
  • Clustering of node iCi=2Liki(ki−1)where Li is the number of links between i's neighbours, and ki(ki−1)/2 is the number of pairs of neighbours. Ci runs from 0 (no friends know each other) to 1 (all do).

  • Eigenvector centralityA𝐱=λmax⁡𝐱where 𝐱 lists every node's score and λmax⁡ is the largest eigenvalue of A. A node scores high if its neighbours do.
  • Complete graph, treeN(N−1)/2 links, and N−1 linkswhere a complete graph has every pair linked, and a tree is connected with no loops.
  • Euler's methodyn+1=yn+hf(yn)where the equation is y˙=f(y), h is the step size, and yn is the estimate after n steps.

The two questions behind most proofs

Nearly every proof after class 4 is answering one of two questions. Spot which one, and you already know how the proof goes.

Question 1 · Is it stable?

Nudge the system a little. If it comes back, it's stable. If it runs away, it's unstable. The test is always "look at the slope at the fixed point". Only the kind of slope changes.

Kind of systemStable when
1-D flow x˙=f(x)f′(x∗)<0
2-D or 3-D flowEvery eigenvalue of the Jacobian has negative real part (in 2-D, τ<0 and Δ>0)
Map xn+1=f(xn)|f′(x∗)|<1
Large random systemSCσ2<d (May)

Why is a map different? A flow adds a little bit each instant, so the sign of the slope matters. A map multiplies the error by f′ every step, so its size matters. Multiply by 0.5 over and over and you shrink to nothing. Multiply by 2 and you blow up.

Question 2 · Where is the threshold?

Most "critical values" in the course come from one ratio reaching 1. Below it, nothing happens. Above it, something takes off.

What happensThreshold
A giant component appears⟨k⟩=1
A disease (SIS) spreadsβ/γ=1
A disease spreads on a networkβκ1/γ=1
A map's fixed point stops being stable|f′(x∗)|=1 (logistic map, r=3)
The Lorenz origin stops being stableρ=1
Two oscillators lock together|Δω|/(k1+k2)=1, because sin⁡ϕ can't go past 1
A large system stops being stableSCσ2/d=1

For flows the threshold is a slope reaching 0 instead: the Hopf bifurcation happens at μ=0, and every 1-D bifurcation happens where f′=0. The power-law exponents 2 and 3 are the odd ones out. They're where ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩ stop being finite.

One recipe for stability

This one recipe is the skeleton of the SIS proof, every bifurcation question, rabbits vs sheep, predator–prey, Lorenz, the Hopf question and the two-oscillator question. Learn it once and you have the outline of half the paper.

  1. Find the fixed points. Set every rate to zero and solve.
  2. Take the slope. In 1-D that's f′(x). In 2-D or 3-D it's the Jacobian J.
  3. Put each fixed point in. In 1-D read the sign. In 2-D work out τ and Δ.
  4. Say what it means. "Stable, so the disease settles at 1−γ/β", or "saddle, so the species can't coexist".

For a map, step 3 checks |f′|<1 instead. Even the two-oscillator question is a 1-D problem in disguise, because the phase difference ϕ obeys a single equation.

Show that ∑iki=2L (the handshaking lemma), and that the number of odd-degree nodes is even.

Count the link-ends two ways.

  • Total link-ends =2Levery link has exactly two ends
  • Total link-ends =∑ikinode i holds ki of them
  • So ∑iki=2Lsame thing counted twice
  • 2L is even, so an even number of the ki are odda sum is even only if it has an even number of odd terms

Full proof: Quiz 1 guide §3

Show that [An]ij is the number of walks of length n from i to j.

Matrix multiplication sums over the middle node, and AikAkj=1 only when i→k→j is a real walk.

  • [A2]ij=∑kAikAkjrule for multiplying matrices
  • AikAkj=1 exactly when i→k→j is a walk, else 0both links must exist
  • So [A2]ij counts 2-step walkssumming over every middle node k
  • [An]ij=∑k[An−1]ikAkj(n−1)-step walks to k, then one last step. Repeat (induction).

Full proof: Quiz 1 guide §4

Show that the number of triangles is 16tr⁡(A3).

A triangle is a closed walk of length 3, and each triangle gets counted 6 times.

  • [A3]ii = closed 3-step walks from iwalk-counting result above
  • A closed 3-walk i→a→b→i is a triangleno self-loops, so the three nodes are different and all linked
  • tr⁡(A3)=∑i[A3]ii = all closed 3-walks
  • Each triangle gives 3×2=6 of them3 starting corners, 2 directions
  • Triangles =16tr⁡(A3)

Full proof: Quiz 1 guide §4

Show that an Euler circuit needs every degree even, and an Euler trail needs exactly 2 odd nodes.

Every time the trail walks into a node, it walks out on a new link, so links get used in pairs.

  • At a middle node, links are used in (in, out) pairsarrive on one link, leave on another unused one
  • kv=2× (number of visits), which is evenevery link is used exactly once
  • Start node: one extra "out". End node: one extra "in". Both odd.
  • Closed trail: start = end, the extras pair up, so all even
  • So circuit means 0 odd nodes, open trail means exactly 2 (its ends), more than 2 means neither

Full proof: Quiz 1 guide §5

On a path of N nodes, show that the node at position i has betweenness (i−1)(N−i).

There's only one route between any two nodes, and it goes through you exactly when they're on opposite sides of you.

  • Every pair has exactly one shortest patha path graph has no loops
  • It passes through node i iff one end is left of i and the other is right
  • i−1 nodes on the left, N−i on the right
  • Betweenness =(i−1)(N−i)each such pair adds 1/1=1

Full method: Quiz 1 guide §8

Show that the forward difference has error O(h) and the central difference O(h2).

Taylor-expand. In the central difference, stepping forward and back makes the h2 terms cancel.

  • f(x0±h)=f(x0)±hf′+h22f′′±h36f′′′+…Taylor series
  • f(x0+h)−f(x0)h=f′+h2f′′+…, so error O(h)
  • f(x0+h)−f(x0−h)=2hf′+h33f′′′+…subtract. f and f′′ terms cancel.
  • f(x0+h)−f(x0−h)2h=f′+h26f′′′+…, so error O(h2)

Solve u˙=a−bu (this is also the charging capacitor).

Separate the variables and integrate. The answer slides exponentially to the fixed point a/b.

  • u∗=a/b, stablea−bu∗=0, and slope −b<0
  • dua−bu=dtseparate
  • −1bln⁡a−bu(t)a−bu(0)=tintegrate from 0 to t
  • u(t)=ab+(u(0)−ab)e−bt
  • Capacitor: V0=q/C+Rq˙ gives a=V0/R, b=1/RC, so q(t)=CV0(1−e−t/RC)

For P(k)=Ck−γ, find C, and say when it is finite.

Probabilities add up to 1. Treat k as continuous and integrate.

  • ∫kmin⁡∞Ck−γdk=1total probability is 1
  • C[k1−γ1−γ]kmin⁡∞=1

Find ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩ for a power law, and show that ⟨k²⟩ diverges for γ≤3.

Same integral with one more k each time. Each extra k raises the γ you need by 1.

  • ⟨k⟩=C∫kmin⁡∞k1−γdk=γ−1γ−2kmin⁡finite only if γ>2

  • γ>3: ⟨k2⟩=γ−1γ−3kmin⁡2
  • Real networks have 2<γ<3: finite ⟨k⟩, infinite ⟨k²⟩

Full proof: Mid-sem guide §1

Explain why linear bins give a noisy tail on a log-log plot, and why log bins fix it.

The tail has very few nodes, so equal-width bins there hold 0 or 1 node. Make the bins wider as k grows.

  • log⁡P=log⁡C−γlog⁡k, a straight line of slope −γtake logs of Ck−γ
  • Linear bins: tail bins get 0 or 1 nodeP(k) is tiny for large k
  • So the tail is noisy, flattens into a floor, and zeros can't be plottedlog⁡0 is undefined
  • Log bins grow by a fixed factor (1, 2, 4, 8, …), so wide tail bins collect enough nodes
  • Divide each count by N× bin width, and plot at the bin centre

Full answer: Mid-sem guide §2

Star with N nodes: show that ⟨l⟩=2(N−1)/N and that every clustering coefficient is 0.

Only two kinds of pair, hub–leaf (distance 1) and leaf–leaf (distance 2). No two leaves are linked, so no triangles.

  • N−1 hub–leaf pairs at distance 1directly linked
  • (N−1)(N−2)2 leaf–leaf pairs at distance 2only route is through the hub
  • Total distance =(N−1)+(N−1)(N−2)=(N−1)2
  • ⟨l⟩=(N−1)2N(N−1)/2=2(N−1)N→2divide by the number of pairs
  • Chub=0, Cleaf=0, so ⟨C⟩=0no two leaves linked, and a leaf has one neighbour. It's a tree.

Ring of N nodes: show that ⟨l⟩≈N/4, and ≈N/(2k) when each node has degree k.

From any node, the distances run 1, 2, …, up to about N/2, two nodes at each. Their average is about N/4.

  • From one node: 2 nodes at each distance 1,2,…,N/2go either way round
  • Average of 1,…,N/2 ≈N4half the largest value
  • Every node looks the same, so ⟨l⟩≈N/4
  • Degree k: each step covers k/2 places, so ⟨l⟩≈N/4k/2=N2kk=2 gives back N/4

Show that the ER degree distribution becomes Poisson for large N and small p. Find ⟨k⟩ and ⟨k²⟩.

Start from the binomial, keep ⟨k⟩ fixed, and approximate each of its three factors.

  • P(k)=(N−1k)pk(1−p)N−1−kchoose which k of the N−1 links exist, each with probability p
  • (N−1k)≈(N−1)kk!k≪N
  • pk=⟨k⟩k(N−1)kp=⟨k⟩/(N−1)
  • (1−p)N−1−k≈e−⟨k⟩(1−x/n)n→e−x

  • ⟨k⟩ =p(N−1), variance = ⟨k⟩, so ⟨k2⟩=⟨k⟩+⟨k⟩2

Derive the giant-component equation S=1−e−⟨k⟩S.

A node is outside the giant component if each other node is either not linked to it, or linked but outside too.

Show that a giant component exists if and only if ⟨k⟩>1, and that β=1.

Draw y=S and y=1−e−⟨k⟩S. The curve starts with slope ⟨k⟩ and bends down, so it can rise above the line only if ⟨k⟩ > 1.

  • S=0 is always a solution1−e0=0

Show that an ER graph has ⟨l⟩≈ln⁡N/ln⁡⟨k⟩.

You reach ⟨k⟩ nodes in one step, ⟨k⟩² in two, ⟨k⟩d in d. That explodes, so very few steps reach everyone.

  • About ⟨k⟩r nodes at distance rthe neighbourhood is tree-like (no nearby loops)
  • N∼1+⟨k⟩+⋯+⟨k⟩d≈⟨k⟩dthe last term dominates
  • ln⁡N≈dln⁡⟨k⟩take logs
  • ⟨l⟩≈d≈ln⁡Nln⁡⟨k⟩, which grows slowly (concave in N)

Full proof: Mid-sem guide §6

Show that in an ER graph Ci=p for every degree, and sketch C‾ against N.

Whether two of your friends are linked is just another coin flip with probability p.

  • Ci=ei/(ki2)linked neighbour pairs over all neighbour pairs
  • E[ei]=p(ki2)each pair is linked independently with probability p
  • E[Ci]=p, and ki cancels
  • C‾=p≈⟨k⟩/N⟨k⟩≈Np
